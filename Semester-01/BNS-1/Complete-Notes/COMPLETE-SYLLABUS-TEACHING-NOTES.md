# BNS-1 Complete Syllabus Teaching Notes
**CCSU LL.B. — Paper IV: Law of Crimes (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023), Paper Code K-1006**

**Audience:** First-semester beginner. **Purpose:** Learn the syllabus directly from these notes; this is not merely a link directory.

> **Legal-use note.** These are study explanations and section references, not a certified verbatim reprint of every statutory provision. Before submitting a legal opinion or quoting statutory language in an exam, check the exact current text. BNS came into force on 1 July 2024 except BNS section 106(2), which was excluded from the commencement notification and remains uncommenced on the latest check made for this edition (20 October 2026). Case law under the IPC is identified as legacy authority; apply it to BNS only where the statutory language and principle remain relevant.

## How to study each topic
For every answer, do five things: (1) give the BNS section and definition; (2) list its ingredients; (3) explain the mental element (intention, knowledge, dishonesty, fraudulence, rashness or negligence); (4) apply the rule to the facts; (5) state a reasoned conclusion. For a long answer, add one or two relevant cases and distinguish the cases if necessary.

## 1. Meaning and elements of crime

A **crime** is conduct prohibited by criminal law and made punishable by the State. Not every immoral, rude or harmful act is a crime. The prosecution must establish the ingredients of a defined offence, subject to the rules of evidence and any statutory presumptions.

### Core elements
1. **Human conduct:** An act or legally blameworthy omission. An omission is criminal only where a legal duty to act exists.
2. **Actus reus (external element):** The act/omission, circumstances and any required consequence specified in the offence.
3. **Mens rea (mental element):** The state of mind required by the offence—intention, knowledge, dishonesty, fraudulence, rashness or negligence, as appropriate.
4. **Causation:** Where a consequence is required (such as death or injury), the prosecution must connect the conduct to that consequence according to the applicable legal test.
5. **Concurrence:** Generally, the required mental element must accompany the prohibited conduct, unless the statutory structure provides otherwise.
6. **Absence of a valid defence:** The act may be excused or justified by a General Exception, such as a legally recognised right of private defence.
7. **Punishment by law:** A person cannot be punished for conduct merely because it appears wrong; an applicable law must define the offence and punishment.

**Example:** A person accidentally breaks a shop window while lawfully playing cricket. Property damage exists, but the prosecution still must establish the required offence ingredients and mental element. The word “accident” is not automatically a defence; BNS section 18 must be tested against the facts.

### Mens rea and important mental states
- **Intention:** The person consciously aims to bring about a consequence or do the prohibited act.
- **Knowledge:** The person is aware of a relevant fact or likely consequence, even if that consequence is not their desired goal.
- **Dishonestly:** In BNS section 2, the central idea is intention to cause wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another.
- **Fraudulently:** Doing something with intent to defraud; the exact statutory definition and context matter.
- **Rashness:** Conscious taking of an unjustifiable risk, often described as acting despite awareness of danger.
- **Negligence:** Failure to exercise legally required care; criminal negligence must meet the criminal-law threshold, not merely prove that something went wrong.
- **Strict liability:** For some regulatory offences, the statute may exclude the need to prove a usual mental element. This is an exception, not a rule that every crime has no mens rea.

### General rule about mens rea
Courts ordinarily presume that mens rea is required unless the legislation, read as a whole and in context, excludes it. In an exam, do not write “mens rea is always necessary” as an absolute statement. Explain the statutory wording and purpose.

**Legacy cases:** *Nathulal v State of Madhya Pradesh*, AIR 1966 SC 43, supports the importance of mens rea where the statute does not clearly exclude it. *State of Maharashtra v Mayer Hans George*, AIR 1965 SC 722, illustrates that the mental-element requirement can be displaced in a particular regulatory offence by the statutory scheme. These were IPC-era decisions, not decisions interpreting BNS section wording.

## 2. Stages of crime

### (a) Intention
The person thinks about committing the offence. Bare intention is ordinarily not punishable unless a statute creates a specific offence.

### (b) Preparation
The person arranges means or makes plans: for example, buying a tool allegedly intended for a later burglary. Preparation is usually not punishable as an attempt, although certain preparations are separately criminalised by statute.

### (c) Attempt
The person has the intention to commit a specific offence and performs an act that moves sufficiently close to its commission. The act must go beyond mere preparation. Whether it is an attempt depends on the offence and the facts, not on a mechanical “last act” formula.

### (d) Completion
All legally required ingredients of the offence are present. The particular offence may be complete even if the offender does not achieve every hoped-for result, where the statute defines it that way.

**Case:** *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub*, (1980) 3 SCC 57, is a leading preparation/attempt authority. The Court considered conduct in moving silver to a coastal point for unlawful export; acts beyond preparation and sufficiently proximate to completion could amount to an attempt. *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar*, AIR 1961 SC 1698, is another leading authority on the attempt threshold.

**Exam distinction:** Preparation asks, “Were the means being arranged?” Attempt asks, “Had the accused begun executing the offence by an act sufficiently proximate to its commission?”

## 3. Joint and constructive liability

### Common intention — BNS section 3(5)
Where a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the **common intention** of all, each may be liable as if the act were done by that person alone. The prosecution must prove participation and a shared intention; presence at the scene or similar intentions by themselves are not enough. Common intention may be inferred from conduct and circumstances, and it can develop at the scene, but it must be established from evidence.

### Common object and unlawful assembly — BNS sections 189–190
Section 189 defines unlawful assembly and specifies its statutory requirements, including the minimum number of persons and one of the prohibited common objects. Section 190 concerns liability of members for an offence committed in prosecution of the assembly’s common object, or an offence members knew was likely to be committed in prosecution of that object. Common object is not the same as common intention: it need not be proved in the same way as a pre-arranged common intention, and membership and the statutory conditions matter.

| Common intention | Common object |
|---|---|
| BNS section 3(5) | BNS sections 189–190 |
| Shared intention relating to the criminal act | Statutorily defined object of an unlawful assembly |
| Participation plus the shared intention must be proved | Membership, unlawful assembly, and the statutory relationship between offence and common object/knowledge must be proved |
| Can involve two or more persons | Section 189 requires the statutory minimum of five persons |

**Cases:** *Mahbub Shah v King-Emperor*, AIR 1945 PC 118, warns against confusing similar intention with common intention. *Pandurang v State of Hyderabad*, AIR 1955 SC 216, explains the need to prove common intention. *Lalji v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1989) 1 SCC 437, is an important authority on common-object liability under the former IPC provision. Use the cases as principle-based legacy authorities, and cite current BNS sections in the answer.

### Constructive liability
Constructive liability means the law attributes an offence or consequence to a participant because the statutory conditions for group liability are satisfied, even if that participant did not personally perform every physical act. It is not automatic guilt by association. Always identify the particular provision and prove its conditions.

## 4. Inchoate offences: abetment, conspiracy and attempt

### Abetment — BNS sections 45–60
Abetment ordinarily involves one of the legally recognised modes: **instigating** a person to do a thing; engaging in a conspiracy with the legally required act/omission in pursuance of it; or **intentionally aiding** the thing by an act or illegal omission. The exact provisions govern the required conduct, the principal offence, and punishment where the offence is or is not committed.

- Instigation requires more than a vague association; look for intentional encouragement, provocation or urging assessed in context.
- Aiding must be intentional and legally connected to the offence; mere presence is not enough by itself.
- The abettor need not always be the person who physically carries out the main offence.
- Ask whether the underlying act occurred and whether the relevant abetment provision changes punishment.

### Criminal conspiracy — BNS section 61
The offence focuses on an agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act, or a legal act by illegal means, subject to the statutory distinction between agreements that themselves complete the offence and cases requiring an act besides the agreement in pursuance of it. Conspiracy is often proved by surrounding circumstances because agreements are rarely made openly. Suspicion, friendship or being present together is not proof by itself.

### Attempt — BNS section 62 (general provision)
Section 62 addresses attempts where no express provision for punishment of the attempt is made elsewhere. Where a specific offence has its own attempt provision—for example, attempt to murder under section 109—use that specific provision rather than automatically defaulting to section 62.

**Short answer structure:** define the offence → give its BNS section → identify conduct and mental element → distinguish it from preparation/association → apply the facts.

## 5. General Exceptions — BNS sections 14–44

A General Exception does not mean the conduct never happened. It means that, if the specific legal conditions are proved, criminal responsibility is excluded or limited. The prosecution must prove the offence as required by law; the accused may rely on a General Exception and bears the evidentiary burden applicable under the evidence law. In case-problem answers, state both the relevant exception and its limits.

### Mistake of fact; not mistake of law
A qualifying mistake of **fact** can protect a person who, in good faith, believes they are bound or justified by law because of the mistaken factual situation. Ignorance or misunderstanding of the law is generally not a defence. “Good faith” has a legal meaning and is not established merely by saying that the person believed they were right.

**Example:** A person takes a bag honestly believing it is their own due to identical bags being exchanged, and there is evidence supporting the honest belief. This may affect the dishonest intention required for theft. It is different from knowing the bag belongs to another but believing that taking it is legally permitted.

### Accident — section 18
The provision concerns an accident or misfortune occurring without criminal intention or knowledge while doing a lawful act in a lawful manner, by lawful means, with proper care and caution. An event being unintended is not enough: lawful conduct and due care are central conditions.

### Necessity — section 19
A person may be protected when, without criminal intent, they do an act knowing it is likely to cause harm but do it in good faith to prevent or avoid other harm to person or property. It requires a real necessity and evaluation of the circumstances. It is not a licence to cause any harm for a merely convenient purpose.

### Children — sections 20–21
BNS section 20 addresses a child under seven years; section 21 addresses a child above seven and under twelve who has not attained sufficient maturity to judge the nature and consequences of the conduct on that occasion. Age and, for section 21, maturity are essential factual issues. Do not confuse this substantive criminal-law exception with separate juvenile-justice procedures.

### Unsoundness of mind — section 22
The question is whether, **at the time of the act**, unsoundness of mind made the person incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that it was wrong or contrary to law, as the section specifies. A medical diagnosis alone does not automatically establish the legal defence; the legal test and evidence at the relevant time matter.

**Case:** *Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v State of Gujarat*, AIR 1964 SC 1563, discusses the insanity defence and evidential burden under the former law. Apply it carefully to BNS section 22 and current evidence law.

### Intoxication — sections 23–24
Involuntary intoxication that satisfies the statutory conditions may excuse responsibility where it produces the required incapacity. Voluntary intoxication is not a general defence. Section 24 deals with offences requiring particular knowledge or intention and contains specific treatment of intoxication. Never answer simply, “The accused was drunk, so there is no crime.”

**Case:** *Basdev v State of PEPSU*, AIR 1956 SC 488, is a classic legacy authority on intoxication and intention/knowledge.

### Consent and other exceptions
Sections 25–30 deal with different consent-based situations and limits; the legal requirements differ by category, including age, capacity, intention, and the nature or likelihood of harm. Consent is not a blanket defence, particularly for offences where the statute expressly excludes or restricts it. Other General Exceptions cover compulsion, trivial harm and related situations. Always quote the exact section relevant to the facts.

### Right of private defence — sections 34–44
The law recognises defensive action for body and property, subject to statutory conditions. It is protective, not punitive.

For an exam, ask:
1. Was there a reasonable apprehension of legally relevant danger to the body or property?
2. Was the right available against this kind of threat and actor?
3. When did the right begin and end?
4. Was the harm inflicted within the statutory limits, or did the defence exceed those limits?
5. Is there evidence of good faith, immediacy, and whether effective recourse to public authorities was realistically available?

There is no rigid rule that a defender must always retreat; nor is the right unlimited. Necessity and proportionality depend on the applicable provision and facts.

**Case:** *Darshan Singh v State of Punjab*, (2010) 2 SCC 333, summarises principles of private defence, including reasonable apprehension and the requirement not to treat the right as revenge. This remains a legacy authority to be read with BNS sections 34–44.

## 6. Offences affecting the human body

### Culpable homicide and murder — sections 100–105
**Section 100 (culpable homicide)** concerns causing death by an act done with the intention of causing death, or bodily injury likely to cause death, or with the knowledge that the act is likely to cause death, as the statutory language specifies.

**Section 101 (murder)** identifies circumstances in which culpable homicide is elevated to murder. It contains statutory exceptions; the exception analysis can change the legal classification. Avoid the simplistic statement that every intentional killing is murder without exceptions.

**Sections 103–105** contain principal punishments for murder and culpable homicide not amounting to murder. Check the exact provision and any applicable sub-section for the facts.

The best comparison is by degrees of intention/knowledge, probability and seriousness of harm, the statutory murder clauses, and the exceptions—not by asking only whether the person died.

**Cases:**
- *State of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya*, (1976) 4 SCC 382: culpable homicide is the genus and murder is its species; the degree of probability and statutory clauses matter.
- *Virsa Singh v State of Punjab*, AIR 1958 SC 465: under the former IPC murder clause relating to bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, the prosecution had to prove the injury, its nature, intention to inflict that particular injury, and that the injury was sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course. Use as a principled analogy only after matching the wording with BNS section 101.
- *K.M. Nanavati v State of Maharashtra*, AIR 1962 SC 605: a leading legacy case on grave and sudden provocation and the statutory exception; do not treat provocation as an automatic defence.

### Death by rash or negligent act — section 106
Section 106(1) deals with causing death by rash or negligent act not amounting to culpable homicide, with a distinct treatment for a registered medical practitioner performing a medical procedure. Criminal negligence requires the legal threshold; a bad outcome alone is not enough. **Section 106(2) is not in force as of this edition’s 20 October 2026 check**, because the commencement notification excluded it. Do not state its enhanced punishment as an operative punishment without a new commencement notification.

### Hurt and grievous hurt — sections 114–125
Section 114 defines hurt; section 115 addresses voluntarily causing hurt; section 116 defines grievous hurt; section 117 addresses voluntarily causing grievous hurt. Later provisions in this group address aggravated means and circumstances, including dangerous weapons and specific purposes. The statute—not a lay description—determines whether an injury is grievous. For an injury problem, name the statutory category that the proved facts satisfy, then select the punishment provision.

### Wrongful restraint and wrongful confinement — sections 126–127
- **Wrongful restraint:** voluntarily obstructing a person so as to prevent them from proceeding in a direction in which they have a right to proceed, subject to the statutory wording and exception.
- **Wrongful confinement:** wrongful restraint that prevents a person from proceeding beyond certain circumscribing limits.
- Confinement is generally the more restrictive form because movement is blocked within defined boundaries.

**Example:** Blocking a person's path across a street may raise restraint. Locking the person inside a room and preventing them from leaving may raise confinement. Apply the actual evidence about boundaries, intention, authority and duration.

### Force, criminal force and assault — sections 128–131 and related punishment provisions
Force, criminal force and assault are separately defined. Criminal force involves use of force with the required intention or knowledge and without consent, in the statutorily described circumstances. Assault can be complete through a gesture or preparation that causes a person to apprehend imminent criminal force; actual physical contact is not always necessary. Identify the correct definition before discussing punishment.

### Kidnapping and abduction — sections 137–138 and related provisions
Kidnapping includes statutory forms such as taking a person beyond India without consent where required, or taking/enticing a child or person of unsound mind out of the keeping of the lawful guardian without that guardian’s consent, subject to the statutory wording and exceptions. **Abduction** focuses on compelling by force, or inducing by deceitful means, a person to go from a place. In problem answers, check the victim's age, who consented, lawful guardianship, means used, and the intended purpose.

## 7. Offences against property

### Theft — section 303
Core questions: Was there movable property? Was it in another person's possession? Was it moved without that person's consent? Was the movement dishonest? A temporary taking can still be theft if the legal elements of dishonest taking are satisfied; “I returned it later” does not automatically answer the question.

**Case:** *K.N. Mehra v State of Rajasthan*, AIR 1957 SC 369, addresses dishonest taking and temporary use of an aircraft. *Pyare Lal Bhargava v State of Rajasthan*, AIR 1963 SC 1094, is another legacy authority on temporary removal of a file and dishonest intention.

### Snatching — section 304
BNS separately recognises snatching as a particular form of sudden or forcible taking from a person or their possession, subject to the section’s wording. Do not automatically use “theft” as the only label if the facts squarely fit section 304.

### Extortion — section 308
Extortion involves intentionally putting a person in fear of injury to that person or another and thereby dishonestly inducing delivery of property or valuable security, or the relevant signed/sealed document, as specified by the statute. Distinguish it from theft: theft is taking/moving property without consent; extortion uses fear to induce delivery.

### Robbery — section 309
Robbery is an aggravated form of theft or extortion meeting the statutory conditions, especially the connection with violence, attempted violence, wrongful restraint or fear of instant harm. Not every theft involving a threat automatically satisfies all robbery ingredients; link the violence/fear to the taking in the manner required by the section.

### Dacoity — section 310
Dacoity is robbery committed or attempted by the statutory minimum of **five or more persons conjointly**, including the relevant persons present and aiding as the provision states. The count and participation facts are critical. A group of four may commit robbery but does not satisfy the numerical ingredient of dacoity under this definition.

### Dishonest misappropriation — section 314
This involves dishonest misappropriation or conversion of movable property to one's own use. Unlike theft, initial possession may be innocent; the later dishonest conversion can be the key fact. Identify when and how the dishonest intention arose.

### Criminal breach of trust — section 316
The central ingredient is **entrustment** of property or dominion over property, followed by dishonest misappropriation, conversion, use or disposal contrary to law or a legal contract, or a direction governing the trust. Mere failure to pay a debt or breach of contract is not automatically criminal breach of trust; entrustment plus dishonest dealing must be shown.

### Cheating — section 318
Cheating requires deception and the legally relevant fraudulent or dishonest inducement, with the consequences specified by the section. For property delivery or alteration/destruction of valuable security, identify the deception and the resulting inducement. A later contractual breach alone does not prove that dishonest intention existed when the representation was made.

**Case:** *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar*, (2000) 4 SCC 168, explains the distinction between cheating and a mere breach of contract, particularly the need to examine dishonest intention at the inception of the transaction under the former IPC provision. Match the principle to BNS section 318.

## 8. Offences against the State and public tranquillity

### Acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity — section 152
Section 152 addresses specified conduct endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, including acts described in the section as exciting or attempting to excite secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, separatist activities, or endangering sovereignty/unity/integrity, and other covered conduct. The precise wording, explanation and statutory exceptions matter. Do **not** simply label section 152 “the same as sedition under IPC section 124A”: the former IPC provision was not carried forward verbatim, and the wording and constitutional issues require separate analysis. A case decided under former section 124A cannot automatically decide every question under section 152.

### Unlawful assembly — section 189; liability — section 190
An assembly must meet the statutory minimum of five persons and one of the prohibited common objects in section 189. The object and the facts showing membership are crucial. Section 190 can extend liability for an offence committed in prosecution of the common object, or one members knew was likely to be committed in prosecution of that object. Mere presence in a crowd is not identical to proving membership with the required facts.

### Rioting — section 191
Rioting involves force or violence by an unlawful assembly, or by a member of it, in prosecution of the common object. Prove the unlawful assembly first, then connect the force/violence to the relevant object. Mere loud argument or a large crowd, without the statutory ingredients, is not enough by itself.

### Affray — section 194
Affray addresses fighting between two or more persons in a public place that disturbs public peace, according to the section’s wording. Distinguish affray from rioting: affray focuses on the fighting and public peace ingredients; rioting depends on an unlawful assembly and the statutory use of force/violence.

**Case:** *Lalji v State of Uttar Pradesh*, (1989) 1 SCC 437, is a leading legacy authority on common-object liability; *Masalti v State of Uttar Pradesh*, AIR 1965 SC 202, discusses proof and liability relating to unlawful assembly. Match old IPC provisions to the BNS text before relying on them.

## 9. Offences against women and children

This area requires exact statutory language, age, consent, relationship and circumstance analysis. Never substitute moral judgment or stereotypes for statutory ingredients. Handle examples respectfully and do not blame a victim.

### Rape and connected offences — sections 63–73
Section 63 defines rape; sections 64–71 prescribe punishment for rape and aggravated/repeat circumstances; sections 72–73 address specified disclosure/publication restrictions. For a problem, identify the act alleged, statutory definition, consent as legally defined, age, whether an aggravated circumstance applies, and the relevant punishment provision. **Under the statutory framework, a person below the specified age cannot provide legally effective consent to the act in the way an adult may; use the exact age thresholds and clauses from the current text.** Do not assume all sexual offences are legally classified as rape; check each section.

### Sexual harassment, disrobing, voyeurism, stalking, insult to modesty — sections 74–79
These provisions create distinct offences with different ingredients. Sexual harassment (section 75), assault with intent to disrobe (section 76), voyeurism (section 77), stalking (section 78) and words/gestures intended to insult a woman's modesty (section 79) are not interchangeable. A good answer identifies the exact behaviour, the required intention/knowledge, repetition or other condition where applicable, consent and the statutory exceptions.

### Dowry death and cruelty — sections 80, 85–86
Section 80 covers dowry death and contains specific conditions about a woman's death in the circumstances stated, together with cruelty or harassment for or in connection with dowry soon before death. Section 85 criminalises cruelty by a husband or relative, while section 86 defines cruelty for that group of provisions. The elements and timing matter; do not assume every unnatural death of a married woman is automatically dowry death without proving the conditions.

### Offences against children — sections 93–99 and other applicable provisions
BNS chapter V contains several child-related offences, including exposure/abandonment of a child, using or procuring a child for certain purposes, and selling or buying a child for prostitution, in the particular circumstances defined by each section. Other child-protection statutes may also apply. Confirm the child's age, the accused's act and intent, purpose, statutory relationship, and whether a special law adds separate offences or procedure.

**Case-law approach:** Supreme Court decisions under the former IPC may illustrate general principles, but BNS students must first apply sections 63–99 and the relevant current wording. Do not cite a case name alone: write its material facts, legal issue, holding and the exact principle relevant to the problem.

## 10. High-value distinctions to memorise

| Distinction | Correct comparison |
|---|---|
| Intention vs motive | Intention is the immediate mental purpose/state relevant to the offence; motive is the reason behind it. Motive may be evidence but is not always an ingredient. |
| Preparation vs attempt | Preparation arranges means; attempt begins execution sufficiently close to the intended offence. |
| Common intention vs common object | Section 3(5) requires shared intention and participation; sections 189–190 require an unlawful assembly and statutory common-object/knowledge conditions. |
| Accident vs negligence | Accident exception requires lawful conduct and proper care; negligence concerns breach of the legally required standard of care. |
| Culpable homicide vs murder | Apply the statutory degrees of intention/knowledge, murder clauses and exceptions; murder is not simply “death caused intentionally.” |
| Hurt vs grievous hurt | Hurt is the basic statutory category; grievous hurt is limited to the categories recognised by section 116. |
| Restraint vs confinement | Restraint obstructs movement in a protected direction; confinement limits movement within circumscribed boundaries. |
| Theft vs extortion | Theft involves dishonest taking/moving without consent; extortion uses fear to dishonestly induce delivery. |
| Robbery vs dacoity | Dacoity requires the statutory minimum of five persons conjointly involved in robbery/attempted robbery. |
| Misappropriation vs breach of trust | Misappropriation can involve later dishonest conversion of property; breach of trust additionally requires entrustment/dominion and dishonest dealing in violation of the relevant obligation. |
| Cheating vs breach of contract | Cheating requires deception and the necessary dishonest/fraudulent inducement; a later breach alone does not establish that offence. |
| Rape vs sexual harassment/voyeurism/stalking | Separate statutory definitions and ingredients apply; do not collapse the offences into a general label. |
| Unlawful assembly vs affray | Unlawful assembly requires five or more and a prohibited common object; affray focuses on fighting in a public place disturbing public peace. |

## 11. How to write a 10–15 mark answer

**Introduction:** Define the legal concept and give the correct BNS section.

**Ingredients:** Present each required ingredient in numbered form. Do not mix punishment into the definition.

**Explanation:** Explain the mental element and any necessary distinction.

**Case law:** Give 1–2 relevant cases with facts and ratio. Identify old IPC cases as legacy authorities where appropriate.

**Application:** Tie every important fact in the question to an ingredient or exception. Explain both sides if facts are disputed.

**Conclusion:** State which offence/defence is most likely on the assumed facts and why. If a fact is missing, say what fact would decide the issue.

## 12. Bare-Act-first checklist
- Is the section number a BNS section, not an old IPC number?
- Have I separated definition, offence, exceptions and punishment provisions?
- Have I identified the actual mental element?
- Is there a special sub-section, aggravating circumstance or exception?
- For group liability, did I prove the relevant common intention/common object ingredients?
- For a defence, did I explain both its conditions and its limits?
- Is a cited case decided under the IPC? If yes, is the statutory wording sufficiently similar for the principle to be relevant?
- Have I checked commencement status where a provision has been excluded from commencement?
- Is my question labelled as practice/predicted rather than a verified previous-year question unless the actual paper has been inspected?
