# BNS-1 Practice Problems with Worked Answers
**CCSU LL.B. · Paper IV · Study directly from this file**

These are original practice questions based on the syllabus. **They are not verified previous-year questions (PYQs)** and must not be described as questions that appeared in an actual CCSU exam.

## Part A — Long-answer questions with answer plans

### 1. Explain the essential elements of a crime and the role of mens rea.
**Answer plan:** Define crime; explain conduct/actus reus, required mental element, causation where the offence requires a result, concurrence, absence of a defence, and punishment under law. Discuss intention, knowledge, dishonesty, fraudulence, rashness and negligence. State that mens rea is generally presumed but statutory context can displace it. Use *Nathulal v State of MP* and *State of Maharashtra v Mayer Hans George* to show the two sides. Finish by applying the rule to a short example. Do not say that every crime invariably needs the same mental element.

### 2. Distinguish intention, preparation, attempt and completion.
**Answer plan:** Define all four stages and explain that mere intention is ordinarily not punishable; preparation is normally not attempt unless separately criminalised; attempt requires action beyond preparation sufficiently proximate to commission; completion occurs when the legal ingredients are satisfied. Discuss the fact-sensitive approach in *State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub* and *Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar*. Illustrate using a person who plans a theft, obtains tools, enters the premises and begins taking property.

### 3. Explain common intention and distinguish it from common object.
**Answer plan:** Explain BNS section 3(5), participation, shared intention and proof from circumstances. Then explain unlawful assembly under section 189 and liability under section 190; minimum five persons and prohibited common object are key. Compare the tests, prove membership/participation, and caution against guilt by association. Use *Mahbub Shah*, *Pandurang*, and *Lalji* as legacy authorities.

### 4. Explain abetment, criminal conspiracy and attempt under BNS.
**Answer plan:** Abetment sections 45–60: instigation, intentional aid, and statutory conspiracy mode; conspiracy section 61: agreement and any additional act requirement that applies; attempt section 62: conduct beyond preparation, subject to specific attempt provisions. Compare agreement, encouragement/assistance and direct execution. Use *Mohd. Yakub* for attempt; identify the BNS provision matching the facts.

### 5. Discuss the General Exceptions and explain the right of private defence.
**Answer plan:** Introduce sections 14–44 and distinguish excuse/justification from absence of an offence. Briefly explain mistake of fact, accident, necessity, child incapacity, unsoundness, intoxication, consent limits and private defence. For private defence sections 34–44, discuss reasonable apprehension, commencement/duration, body/property and statutory limits. Use *Darshan Singh*; for insanity use *Dahyabhai*; for intoxication use *Basdev*. Do not assert that a diagnosis or drunkenness automatically excuses an act.

### 6. Distinguish culpable homicide from murder.
**Answer plan:** Start with BNS sections 100 and 101, explain that murder is a more serious category within culpable homicide, then analyse the degrees of intention/knowledge, statutory murder clauses and exceptions. Point to punishment sections 103–105 only after classification. Use *State of AP v Rayavarapu Punnayya*, *Virsa Singh* and, for provocation, *K.M. Nanavati*. Explain why facts relating to weapon, injury, body part, force, prior conduct, intention and medical evidence may matter. A death alone does not answer the classification.

### 7. Explain theft, extortion, robbery and dacoity.
**Answer plan:** Use sections 303, 308, 309 and 310. Theft involves dishonest taking/movement of movable property without consent; extortion induces delivery through fear of injury; robbery adds statutory violence/restraint/instant-fear circumstances to theft or extortion; dacoity requires five or more persons conjointly in the statutory circumstances. Apply the distinctive ingredient of each and do not treat the labels as synonyms.

### 8. Distinguish dishonest misappropriation, criminal breach of trust and cheating.
**Answer plan:** For section 314 ask when property was dishonestly converted; for section 316 prove entrustment/dominion followed by dishonest prohibited dealing; for section 318 prove deception and the necessary inducement/consequence. A mere civil breach is not automatically cheating or breach of trust. Use *R.K. Dalmia* and *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma* as principle-based legacy cases.

### 9. Explain unlawful assembly, rioting and affray.
**Answer plan:** Section 189 defines unlawful assembly, including minimum five people and a listed common object; section 190 sets out specified liability by reason of membership; section 191 concerns force/violence by an unlawful assembly; section 194 concerns fighting in a public place disturbing public peace. Contrast each offence's ingredients. Use *Lalji* and *Masalti* carefully, noting their former-IPC context.

### 10. Explain rape and distinguish it from sexual harassment, voyeurism and stalking.
**Answer plan:** Start with section 63's definition, then the relevant punishment section in sections 64–71, age/aggravating circumstances and the specific offence charged. Distinguish sections 75 (sexual harassment), 77 (voyeurism) and 78 (stalking) by conduct and ingredients. Discuss *State of Punjab v Gurmit Singh* only for its relevant evidentiary principle; do not substitute case law for the statutory definition. Keep the analysis respectful and free of stereotypes.

## Part B — Short notes (write 100–180 words each)

1. **Strict liability:** A statutory form of liability where proof of a conventional mental element may be excluded or modified. It must be derived from the relevant statute, not assumed merely because an offence is regulatory. Contrast *Nathulal* with *Mayer Hans George*.
2. **Constructive liability:** Liability attributed through a defined statutory rule to a participant who meets its conditions, even if the person did not perform every physical act. It is not automatic guilt by presence.
3. **Mistake of fact:** A qualifying factual mistake under the section may justify/excuse conduct; mistake of law generally does not. Identify the legal basis for the belief and the statutory requirement of good faith.
4. **Accident:** Section 18 requires its particular conditions, including lawful conduct and proper care/caution. Unintended harm alone is not enough.
5. **Legal insanity:** Section 22 uses a legal capacity test at the time of the act; diagnosis alone does not settle it. Cite *Dahyabhai* with a note on the IPC context.
6. **Voluntary intoxication:** Not a general defence. Analyse sections 23–24 and the mental element; cite *Basdev*.
7. **Wrongful restraint and confinement:** Sections 126–127; explain obstruction of a protected direction versus confinement within circumscribed limits.
8. **Snatching:** Section 304 separately criminalises a defined form of taking; compare facts with theft under section 303 and use the exact elements.
9. **Dowry death:** Section 80; prove each statutory condition and the required connection with dowry-related cruelty/harassment. *Satbir Singh* explains “soon before” under the former framework as context-dependent, not necessarily immediate.
10. **Section 152:** Study the actual BNS text on acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India. Do not treat it as a simple renumbering of former IPC section 124A.
11. **Punishment and conviction:** Liability, offence classification and sentencing are separate stages. *Bachan Singh* concerns constitutional sentencing principles for the death penalty; it does not establish guilt for murder.
12. **Effect of legacy cases:** A case under IPC can help explain a principle but does not automatically interpret a new or materially changed BNS clause.

## Part C — Hypothetical problem questions with worked answers

### Problem 1: Similar intention or common intention?
A and B argue with C. Without prior discussion, A suddenly strikes C with a stick. B is standing nearby and does not help, encourage, restrain or participate. The prosecution relies only on B's presence.

**Question:** Can B automatically be held jointly liable under BNS section 3(5)?

**Worked answer:** No, not on these facts alone. Section 3(5) requires a criminal act by several persons in furtherance of their common intention and proof of the necessary participation. Mere presence or similar feelings toward C do not themselves prove the shared intention. *Mahbub Shah* and *Pandurang* support the distinction between common intention and similar intention under the former IPC framework. If evidence later shows that B encouraged A, shared the plan or participated in a way that proves the required common intention, the conclusion could change. On the stated evidence alone, the essential joint-liability ingredient is missing.

### Problem 2: Four people rob a shop
Four people threaten a shopkeeper and take cash using violence.

**Question:** Is the offence automatically dacoity?

**Worked answer:** The facts may support robbery under section 309 if the statutory violence/fear connection and theft/extortion ingredients are proved. But section 310's dacoity definition requires the statutory minimum of five or more persons conjointly involved. Four people do not satisfy that numerical element. Other offences may also be established, but the label “dacoity” is not made out on the numbers stated.

### Problem 3: Preparation or attempt?
P plans to steal a laptop, buys gloves, and goes to a building. A guard stops P outside before P reaches the room or begins taking property.

**Worked answer:** The stated acts are strong indicators of preparation, not necessarily attempt. Intent and preparation exist, but the act sufficiently proximate to the theft may be missing. The answer could depend on additional facts—whether P broke in, entered the room, touched or moved the laptop, or took other acts that commence execution. *Mohd. Yakub* requires a fact-sensitive analysis; do not use the “last act” as an absolute rule. Section 62 applies only if no specific attempt provision covers the offence.

### Problem 4: Return of a file
A removes an office file without permission to help a friend gain an improper advantage, then returns it the next day. A argues that there is no theft because the file was returned.

**Worked answer:** Return is relevant evidence, but it does not automatically negate theft. Under section 303, check movable property, possession, movement without consent, and dishonest intention. *Pyare Lal Bhargava* illustrates that temporary removal can amount to theft when the required dishonest elements are present. The court must infer intention from all the evidence, including the purpose of removal.

### Problem 5: Contract not performed
A tells B that a machine will be delivered next week. A honestly expects to receive it from a supplier, but delivery fails and A cannot refund the price. B alleges cheating.

**Worked answer:** Non-performance alone does not establish cheating under section 318. The key questions are whether A deceived B, whether the relevant dishonest/fraudulent intention and inducement existed at the required time, and whether the statutory consequence followed. If A's representation was honestly believed when made and later circumstances caused the breach, the case may be civil rather than cheating. *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma* supports examining intention at the inception, but the final answer turns on evidence.

### Problem 6: Private defence after danger ends
A is attacked by B. A disarms B and B falls, no longer poses a threat. A then repeatedly strikes B to punish B.

**Worked answer:** The initial response may fall within the right of private defence if B's threat and A's response satisfy sections 34–44. But private defence is protective, not punitive. Once the danger ends, continued blows intended as revenge may exceed or fall outside the right. *Darshan Singh* supports analysing the reasonable apprehension and duration of the right; the exact facts and injury determine the result.

### Problem 7: Death from a single knife wound
A stabs B once in the abdomen. B dies. The prosecution says that death makes it murder.

**Worked answer:** Death does not automatically equal murder. Begin with section 100 and determine whether the conduct caused death with the required intention/knowledge. Then test the clauses of section 101 and its exceptions. Examine the weapon, force, body part, nature of injury, medical evidence, circumstances, statements and conduct. *Rayavarapu Punnayya* supplies the classification framework; *Virsa Singh* addresses one particular former-IPC murder clause concerning an intentionally inflicted injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death. The answer depends on the proved ingredients and any applicable exception.

### Problem 8: Unlawful assembly and affray
Seven people assemble outside a shop. Two of them begin fighting in public; the remaining five stand a short distance away and do not share any apparent objective or join the fight.

**Worked answer:** The fact that seven people were present does not automatically prove unlawful assembly. Under section 189, the prosecution must prove the listed common object and membership. Section 190 liability then depends on its statutory conditions. The two people fighting may raise section 194 affray if the public-place and disturbance-of-peace requirements are satisfied. If evidence shows the assembly had a prohibited common object and force was used in its prosecution, section 191 may also be relevant. The conclusion turns on common object, membership, participation and context, not just headcount.

### Problem 9: A person causes death by negligent driving
A driver causes a death through an alleged rash/negligent act, without an intention or knowledge amounting to culpable homicide.

**Worked answer:** Analyse section 106(1): whether the act was rash/negligent, caused death and did not amount to culpable homicide. A bad outcome does not by itself prove criminal negligence; evidence of conduct and causation matters. Do not apply section 106(2)'s enhanced hit-and-run punishment as operative law: that sub-section was excluded from the commencement notification and is uncommenced on the date of this edition's check.

### Problem 10: Dowry-related harassment
A married woman dies in circumstances alleged to be unnatural. The prosecution says her in-laws had demanded dowry several months earlier, but there is no other fact about later treatment or the relationship between the alleged harassment and the death.

**Worked answer:** Section 80 must be applied by ingredients; an unnatural death plus any historical dowry demand is not automatically enough. The statutory time window, dowry-related cruelty/harassment “soon before” death, and a live and proximate link need to be assessed on the full evidence. *Satbir Singh* explains the former-law phrase “soon before” contextually rather than as a fixed immediate period. More facts about timing and continuity of harassment would be needed for a confident conclusion.

### Problem 11: Voluntary intoxication
A voluntarily drinks heavily and later attacks B. A claims that intoxication means A cannot be guilty of any offence.

**Worked answer:** The claim is too broad. Sections 23–24 distinguish legal situations involving involuntary intoxication and offences requiring particular intention/knowledge. Voluntary intoxication is not an automatic defence. The court must identify the mental element required for the offence and evaluate the evidence. *Basdev v State of PEPSU* is the principal legacy authority to discuss.

### Problem 12: Fourteen-year-old acts knowingly
A child aged ten takes property. The evidence is disputed as to whether the child had sufficient maturity to judge the nature and consequences of the act on that occasion.

**Worked answer:** Section 20 addresses a child under seven; the child here is older than seven and under twelve, so section 21's maturity test is relevant. The court must assess sufficient maturity to judge the nature and consequences on that occasion. Age alone does not resolve it within this age band; relevant evidence and the applicable procedural rules must also be considered.

## Part D — Self-test: identify the section before answering

Write the best initial BNS provision beside each prompt, then explain its elements:
1. A person moves another's phone dishonestly without consent — **section 303 (theft)**.
2. A person induces delivery of money by intentionally creating fear of injury — **section 308 (extortion)**.
3. Five or more people conjointly commit robbery — **section 310 (dacoity)**.
4. A person is entrusted with funds and dishonestly converts them — **section 316 (criminal breach of trust)**.
5. A person uses a deceptive representation to induce delivery of property — **section 318 (cheating)**.
6. A person blocks a route in a direction the victim has a right to proceed — **section 126 (wrongful restraint)**, subject to exact elements.
7. A group has a prohibited common object — analyse **section 189** first, then **section 190** if an offence by a member is alleged.
8. An act causes death with the required intention/knowledge — analyse **section 100**, then **section 101** and the relevant punishment provision.
9. A person instigates or intentionally aids another to commit an offence — analyse the applicable **sections 45–60 (abetment)**.
10. A person uses a gesture that causes apprehension of imminent criminal force — consider **section 130 (assault)**, subject to the definition and facts.

## How to improve an answer
When checking yourself, mark each answer for: correct section (1 mark), all ingredients (2), mental element or exception (2), accurate case/principle (2), application to each key fact (2), reasoned conclusion (1). A confident guess with the wrong BNS number is not a full answer.
