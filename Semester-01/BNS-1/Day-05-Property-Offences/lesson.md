# DAY-05 — Detailed Lesson: Property Offences

**Study identifier:** DAY-05  
**Level:** Beginner  
**Question status:** Predicted questions and original practice problems; not verified previous-year questions.

## Learning outcomes

Explain the statutory ingredients of theft, extortion, robbery, dacoity, dishonest misappropriation, criminal breach of trust and cheating, and distinguish similar offences.

## 1. Theft

Theft concerns dishonest taking of movable property out of another person's possession without consent, with the movement of the property required by the statutory definition. Check the full definition and any explanations in the BNS.

When analysing facts, ask:
- Is the item movable property?
- Was it in another person's possession?
- Was it moved as required by the definition?
- Was consent absent?
- Was the required dishonest intention present?

## 2. Extortion

Extortion concerns intentionally putting a person in fear of injury and thereby dishonestly inducing delivery of property or the relevant valuable item, as provided by the statute. Use the exact definition: fear, inducement and the required delivery are central matters to analyse.

## 3. Robbery and dacoity

Robbery is defined through specified circumstances involving theft or extortion together with the additional conduct or fear identified by the statute. Dacoity involves robbery committed or attempted by the minimum number of persons specified in the Act.

Do not reduce robbery to any theft accompanied by force without checking the exact statutory conditions. For dacoity, establish the number of persons and the robbery-related requirements.

## 4. Dishonest misappropriation of property

This offence concerns dishonest misappropriation or conversion of movable property in circumstances defined by the BNS. Analyse how the property came into the person's hands, the change in treatment of the property, dishonest intention, and any relevant statutory provision.

## 5. Criminal breach of trust

Criminal breach of trust generally involves entrustment or dominion over property, followed by dishonest misappropriation, conversion, use or disposal contrary to the relevant legal direction or arrangement, subject to the statute.

The key question is whether the property was entrusted or under the person's dominion and then dishonestly dealt with in the prohibited way.

## 6. Cheating

Cheating centres on deception and the legally required inducement or consequence under the statutory definition. In a problem answer, consider when deception occurred, what representation was made, the accused's intention at the relevant time, what the other person was induced to do or omit, and the resulting harm or delivery specified by law.

A later failure to keep a promise does not by itself prove cheating. The facts must establish the required deception and other statutory ingredients.

## Quick comparison table

| Offence | Central issue to analyse |
|---|---|
| Theft | Dishonest taking of movable property from another's possession without consent |
| Extortion | Fear of injury and dishonest inducement/delivery meeting the statutory definition |
| Robbery | Theft or extortion plus the additional statutory conditions |
| Dacoity | Robbery committed or attempted by the statutory minimum number of persons |
| Criminal breach of trust | Entrustment/dominion followed by prohibited dishonest dealing |
| Cheating | Deception plus the statutory inducement or consequence |

## Case-law anchors for this lesson
- **K.N. Mehra v State of Rajasthan, AIR 1957 SC 369:** temporary use does not by itself rule out theft; test the statutory ingredients and dishonest intention.
- **Pyare Lal Bhargava v State of Rajasthan, AIR 1963 SC 1094:** later return of temporarily removed property does not automatically negate theft if the required dishonest elements are proved.
- **R.K. Dalmia v Delhi Administration, AIR 1962 SC 1821:** criminal breach of trust requires entrustment/dominion and dishonest dealing in the prohibited way.
- **Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar, (2000) 4 SCC 168:** a mere later breach of contract does not automatically amount to cheating; deception, inducement and the relevant intention must be examined.
- These are former-IPC authorities. Match the principle to BNS sections 303–319 and do not assume old penalties or every detail carried over unchanged.
