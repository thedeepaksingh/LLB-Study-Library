# Core BNS Bare-Act Extracts and Ingredient Notes
**CCSU LL.B. · BNS-1 · Read and memorise alongside the full subject notes**

This file contains the actual operative wording of selected high-value BNS provisions, followed by plain-English ingredient notes. Text inside blockquotes is reproduced as statutory wording from the Act's published text. Any line labelled “Teaching note” is an explanation, not statutory language. This is a selected study extract; it does **not** reproduce all 358 BNS sections. Use the full statutory text for any provision not reproduced here, and check the current version before quoting in a legal filing.

## 1. Section 3(5) — Common intention

> When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone.

**Ingredients to remember**
1. A criminal act is done.
2. Several persons are involved.
3. The act is in furtherance of their common intention.
4. Each person's link to the common intention and participation must be established from evidence.

**Teaching note:** Similar intention or presence at the scene is not enough by itself. Learn *Mahbub Shah v King-Emperor* and *Pandurang v State of Hyderabad* for the distinction. These are legacy IPC authorities applied only to the extent the principle carries over.

## 2. Section 18 — Accident in doing a lawful act

> Nothing is an offence which is done by accident or misfortune, and without any criminal intention or knowledge in the doing of a lawful act in a lawful manner by lawful means and with proper care and caution.

**Ingredients:** accident/misfortune; absence of criminal intention or knowledge; lawful act; lawful manner and means; proper care and caution. If carelessness or unlawful conduct is shown, the exception may not apply.

## 3. Section 19 — Act likely to cause harm, but done without criminal intent, and to prevent other harm

> Nothing is an offence merely by reason of its being done with the knowledge that it is likely to cause harm, if it be done without any criminal intention to cause harm, and in good faith for the purpose of preventing or avoiding other harm to person or property.

**Teaching note:** This is a necessity-type exception. Identify the threatened harm, the harm risked, the good-faith purpose and the actual circumstances. It is not a blanket defence for a convenient or disproportionate choice.

## 4. Section 20 — Child under seven years

> Nothing is an offence which is done by a child under seven years of age.

**Teaching note:** This is the absolute age category in the provision. Section 21 deals with a different age band and requires a maturity assessment.

## 5. Section 21 — Child above seven and under twelve of immature understanding

> Nothing is an offence which is done by a child above seven years of age and under twelve, who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge of the nature and consequences of his conduct on that occasion.

**Teaching note:** The relevant age band and the maturity test must both be addressed. Maturity is assessed in relation to the nature and consequences of the conduct on that occasion.

## 6. Section 22 — Act of a person of unsound mind

> Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong or contrary to law.

**Teaching note:** The key phrase is “at the time of doing it.” A diagnostic label does not automatically establish the statutory defence. *Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v State of Gujarat*, AIR 1964 SC 1563, is a classic legacy authority on legal insanity and evidentiary burden.

## 7. Section 100 — Culpable homicide

> Whoever causes death by doing an act with the intention of causing death, or with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or with the knowledge that he is likely by such act to cause death, commits the offence of culpable homicide.

**How to use it:** Prove death and causation first. Then analyse intention/knowledge and the circumstances. Section 101 determines whether that culpable homicide amounts to murder; do not leap directly to a punishment section.

## 8. Section 101 — Murder (main operative clauses)

> Except in the cases hereinafter excepted, culpable homicide is murder,—

> (a) if the act by which the death is caused is done with the intention of causing death; or

> (b) if the act by which the death is caused is done with the intention of causing such bodily injury as the offender knows to be likely to cause the death of the person to whom the harm is caused; or

> (c) if the act by which the death is caused is done with the intention of causing bodily injury to any person and the bodily injury intended to be inflicted is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; or

> (d) if the person committing the act knows that it is so imminently dangerous that it must, in all probability, cause death, or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, and commits such act without any excuse for incurring the risk of causing death or such injury as aforesaid.

### The five statutory exceptions to section 101 — revision summary
The full exception clauses must be read in the Act. In outline, the exceptions concern: (1) grave and sudden provocation, subject to its statutory limits; (2) exceeding the right of private defence in good faith and without premeditation, with no intention to do more harm than necessary; (3) a public servant or person aiding public justice exceeding powers in good faith under the specified conditions; (4) a sudden fight without premeditation, in the heat of passion, without taking undue advantage or acting cruelly/unusually; and (5) a person above eighteen years consenting to suffer death or taking the risk of death in the circumstances stated by law.

**Teaching note:** Do not learn only the labels of the exceptions. Each one contains legal conditions and qualifications. *State of AP v Rayavarapu Punnayya*, (1976) 4 SCC 382, gives the overall culpable-homicide/murder framework. *Virsa Singh v State of Punjab*, AIR 1958 SC 465, concerns one particular clause under the former IPC text.

## 9. Section 103 — Punishment for murder (core rule)

> Whoever commits murder shall be punished with death or imprisonment for life, and shall also be liable to fine.

**Important:** Section 103 also contains a separate sub-section concerning a group of five or more persons acting in concert who commit murder on specified identity-related grounds. Read the exact current sub-section and prove all its elements before discussing it. For the constitutional sentencing framework, learn *Bachan Singh v State of Punjab*, (1980) 2 SCC 684; it concerns sentencing, not the definition of murder.

## 10. Section 105 — Punishment for culpable homicide not amounting to murder (structure)

**Teaching note based on the statutory provision:** The section distinguishes (a) acts done with the intention of causing death or bodily injury likely to cause death and (b) acts done with knowledge that death is likely but without the specified intention. The punishment provisions differ between these categories. In an exam, consult the exact text for the applicable punishment tier; do not collapse both categories into one answer.

## 11. Section 114 — Hurt

> Whoever causes bodily pain, disease or infirmity to any person is said to cause hurt.

**Teaching note:** The legal definition is not restricted to visible wounds. Use section 116 to decide whether a hurt is “grievous hurt”.

## 12. Section 116 — Grievous hurt (the statutory categories)

> The following kinds of hurt only are designated as “grievous”, namely:—

> First.—Emasculation.

> Secondly.—Permanent privation of the sight of either eye.

> Thirdly.—Permanent privation of the hearing of either ear.

> Fourthly.—Privation of any member or joint.

> Fifthly.—Destruction or permanent impairing of the powers of any member or joint.

> Sixthly.—Permanent disfiguration of the head or face.

> Seventhly.—Fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth.

> Eighthly.—Any hurt which endangers life or which causes the sufferer to be during the space of fifteen days in severe bodily pain, or unable to follow his ordinary pursuits.

**Exam warning:** “Grievous” in ordinary speech and “grievous hurt” in criminal law are not the same thing. Fit the injury into one of the statutory categories using medical and other evidence.

## 13. Section 126 — Wrongful restraint

> Whoever voluntarily obstructs any person so as to prevent that person from proceeding in any direction in which that person has a right to proceed, is said wrongfully to restrain that person.

**Teaching note:** Identify the voluntary obstruction and the direction in which the person had a right to proceed, subject to the provision's exception. Confinement is dealt with separately in section 127.

## 14. Section 127 — Wrongful confinement

> Whoever wrongfully restrains any person in such a manner as to prevent that person from proceeding beyond certain circumscribing limits, is said “wrongfully to confine” that person.

**Memory aid:** Restraint restricts a direction; confinement restricts movement beyond boundaries.

## 15. Section 129 — Criminal force

> Whoever intentionally uses force to any person, without that person’s consent, in order to the committing of any offence, or intending by the use of such force to cause, or knowing it to be likely that by the use of such force he will cause injury, fear or annoyance to the person to whom the force is used, is said to use criminal force to that other.

**Teaching note:** Use the statutory definition of “force” in section 128, then apply the intent/knowledge and lack-of-consent conditions in section 129.

## 16. Section 130 — Assault

> Whoever makes any gesture, or any preparation intending or knowing it to be likely that such gesture or preparation will cause any person present to apprehend that he who makes that gesture or preparation is about to use criminal force to that person, is said to commit an assault.

**Teaching note:** Assault can be complete without physical contact, if the legal requirements concerning gesture/preparation and apprehension are proved. Distinguish it from the actual use of criminal force.

## 17. Section 138 — Abduction

> Whoever by force compels, or by any deceitful means induces, any person to go from any place, is said to abduct that person.

**Teaching note:** Compare abduction with kidnapping under section 137. For kidnapping from lawful guardianship, age, custody and guardian consent are important; abduction focuses on force or deceitful inducement to move a person.

## 18. Section 189 — Unlawful assembly (core definition)

> An assembly of five or more persons is designated an “unlawful assembly”, if the common object of the persons composing that assembly is—

> (a) to overawe by criminal force, or show of criminal force, the Central Government or any State Government or Parliament or the Legislature of any State, or any public servant in the exercise of the lawful power of such public servant; or

> (b) to resist the execution of any law, or of any legal process; or

> (c) to commit any mischief or criminal trespass, or other offence; or

> (d) by means of criminal force, or show of criminal force, to any person, to take or obtain possession of any property, or to deprive any person of the enjoyment of a right of way, or of the use of water or other incorporeal right of which he is in possession or enjoyment, or to enforce any right or supposed right; or

> (e) by means of criminal force, or show of criminal force, to compel any person to do what he is not legally bound to do, or to omit to do what he is legally entitled to do.

**Teaching note:** The minimum of five people alone is not sufficient. A statutory common object must also be proved.

## 19. Section 190 — Every member of unlawful assembly guilty of offence committed in prosecution of common object

> If an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who, at the time of the committing of that offence, is a member of the same assembly, is guilty of that offence.

**Teaching note:** The two statutory routes are prosecution of common object or the relevant knowledge of likely commission. Establish membership and connect the offence to the object/knowledge; do not infer guilt just from presence near a crowd.

## 20. Section 191 — Rioting (core rule)

> Whenever force or violence is used by an unlawful assembly, or by any member thereof, in prosecution of the common object of such assembly, every member of such assembly is guilty of the offence of rioting.

**Teaching note:** Prove the unlawful assembly and the force/violence connection. Compare section 194 (affray), which has different ingredients.

## 21. Section 194 — Affray

> When two or more persons, by fighting in a public place, disturb public peace, they are said to commit an affray.

**Teaching note:** The core points are two or more persons, fighting, a public place and disturbance of public peace.

## 22. Section 303 — Theft (definition and punishment extract)

> Whoever, intending to take dishonestly any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person’s consent, moves that property in order to such taking, is said to commit theft.

The section's explanations describe, among other matters, when a thing attached to the earth becomes capable of being stolen, when a person causes a thing to move, how an animal's movement is treated, and how express or implied consent matters.

> Whoever commits theft shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three years, or with fine, or with both and in case of second or subsequent conviction of any person under this section, he shall be punished with rigorous imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than one year but which may extend to five years and with fine:

> Provided that in cases of theft where the value of the stolen property is less than five thousand rupees, and a person is convicted for the first time, shall upon return of the value of property or restoration of the stolen property, shall be punished with community service.

**Teaching note:** For the community-service proviso, all conditions matter: value less than ₹5,000, first conviction, and return/restoration. Do not write that every low-value theft automatically results in community service.

## 23. Section 308 — Extortion (definition)

> Whoever intentionally puts any person in fear of any injury to that person, or to any other, and thereby dishonestly induces the person so put in fear to deliver to any person any property, or valuable security or anything signed or sealed which may be converted into a valuable security, commits extortion.

**Teaching note:** The fear must be linked to dishonest inducement and delivery. Compare section 303: theft does not depend on the victim being induced by fear to deliver the property.

## 24. Section 316 — Criminal breach of trust (definition)

> Whoever, being in any manner entrusted with property, or with any dominion over property, dishonestly misappropriates or converts to his own use that property, or dishonestly uses or disposes of that property in violation of any direction of law prescribing the mode in which such trust is to be discharged, or of any legal contract, express or implied, which he has made touching the discharge of such trust, or wilfully suffers any other person so to do, commits criminal breach of trust.

**Teaching note:** Memorise **entrustment/dominion + dishonest dealing**. A debtor's failure to repay a loan is not by itself proof of criminal breach of trust; identify the entrustment and the prohibited dishonest dealing.

## 25. Section 318 — Cheating (core definition)

> Whoever, by deceiving any person, fraudulently or dishonestly induces the person so deceived to deliver any property to any person, or to consent that any person shall retain any property, or intentionally induces the person so deceived to do or omit to do anything which he would not do or omit if he were not so deceived, and which act or omission causes or is likely to cause damage or harm to that person in body, mind, reputation or property, is said to cheat.

**Teaching note:** Identify (1) deception, (2) fraudulent/dishonest inducement or the covered intentional inducement, and (3) the statutory consequence. For a transaction later broken, explain why the necessary dishonest intention did or did not exist at the relevant time. *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar*, (2000) 4 SCC 168, is a legacy authority on this distinction.
