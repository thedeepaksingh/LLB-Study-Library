# BNS-1 Case-Law Casebook
**CCSU LL.B. · Paper IV: Law of Crimes (BNS) · For first-semester students**

Every case below contains (1) facts in brief, (2) issue, (3) decision/principle, and (4) exam use. The citations are included for identification. Many leading criminal-law cases were decided under the Indian Penal Code, 1860. They remain useful for general principles only to the extent the underlying statutory wording and principle carry across; they are not rulings that directly interpret every new BNS provision. Always start with the BNS text.

## A. General principles, mens rea and liability

### 1. Nathulal v State of Madhya Pradesh
**Citation:** AIR 1966 SC 43  
**Syllabus connection:** Concept of crime; mens rea; strict liability; BNS section 2 and the relevant offence provision.

**Facts:** Nathulal was prosecuted under a regulatory licensing order relating to foodgrains. He had applied for a licence, and the dispute concerned whether he could be criminally liable when the licensing position was not known to him in the way alleged by the prosecution.

**Issue:** Does a statutory offence necessarily dispense with a guilty mind merely because it is regulatory in nature?

**Principle:** The Supreme Court treated mens rea as an important presumption in criminal offences unless the statutory language, context and purpose exclude it. On the facts, a genuine lack of the required guilty knowledge mattered.

**How to use it:** Use this case to explain the general presumption that mens rea is normally required. Then qualify the statement: Parliament can create offences in which a conventional mental element is excluded or modified. Do not write that every offence always requires intention.

**BNS caution:** This is an IPC-era/regulatory-offence case, not an interpretation of a specific BNS section.

### 2. State of Maharashtra v Mayer Hans George
**Citation:** AIR 1965 SC 722  
**Syllabus connection:** Strict liability; mens rea; statutory interpretation.

**Facts:** The case involved gold being brought into India contrary to foreign-exchange control requirements. A central issue was the accused's knowledge of the applicable restriction.

**Issue:** Must the prosecution prove knowledge or a guilty mind for every regulatory offence?

**Principle:** The Court interpreted the statutory scheme and held that, in the offence before it, mens rea was not a necessary ingredient in the ordinary way because of the legislation's design and requirements.

**How to use it:** Pair it with *Nathulal*. Together they demonstrate that the mens rea question is resolved by construing the particular statute, not by using an absolute slogan.

**Exam sentence:** “Mens rea is ordinarily presumed, but the presumption can be displaced by the wording, structure and object of the statute.”

## B. Joint liability, common intention and common object

### 3. Barendra Kumar Ghosh v King-Emperor
**Citation:** AIR 1925 PC 1; (1924–25) 52 IA 40  
**Syllabus connection:** Common intention; BNS section 3(5).

**Facts:** The case involved a group robbery in which not every participant personally carried out the same physical act. The accused's role and shared criminal purpose were central issues.

**Issue:** Can joint liability arise when a participant did not personally perform the fatal or principal act?

**Principle:** The case is associated with the principle that a participant may be liable where the statutory conditions for a criminal act done in furtherance of common intention are established. Physical roles can differ; shared intention and participation still must be proved.

**How to use it:** Explain why the law does not always require each participant to perform the whole offence personally. Do not turn the case into a rule that mere presence establishes common intention.

**BNS connection:** Apply BNS section 3(5). The decision arose under former IPC section 34.

### 4. Mahbub Shah v King-Emperor
**Citation:** AIR 1945 PC 118  
**Syllabus connection:** Common intention; distinction from similar intention.

**Facts:** The case concerned a violent incident involving several people, and whether the prosecution had adequately shown a shared common intention among the accused.

**Issue:** Is it enough that several accused have similar intentions or take part in the same incident?

**Principle:** No. Common intention is not the same as several people independently having a similar intention. A pre-arranged plan is a classic way of proving it, although the shared intention can be inferred from the circumstances; the evidence must establish the required common intention.

**How to use it:** Cite when distinguishing group presence or parallel intentions from a shared criminal intention under BNS section 3(5).

### 5. Pandurang v State of Hyderabad
**Citation:** AIR 1955 SC 216  
**Syllabus connection:** Common intention; joint liability.

**Facts:** Several accused were involved in a violent occurrence. The Court considered whether the evidence justified attributing the act to all of them on the basis of common intention.

**Issue:** Can common intention be inferred solely because people shared a similar purpose or were present at an incident?

**Principle:** Similar intention is not automatically common intention. The prosecution must establish the shared intention required for joint liability, through the conduct and surrounding circumstances.

**How to use it:** Use with *Mahbub Shah* for the distinction between “same/similar intention” and “common intention.”

### 6. Lalji v State of Uttar Pradesh
**Citation:** (1989) 1 SCC 437  
**Syllabus connection:** Common object; unlawful assembly; BNS sections 189–190.

**Facts:** The case concerned criminal liability arising from an unlawful assembly and the conduct of its members.

**Issue:** Must every member of an unlawful assembly personally carry out an overt act before liability can attach for an offence linked to the assembly's common object?

**Principle:** Where the statutory conditions for unlawful-assembly liability are established, individual overt action by every member is not a universal prerequisite. Membership, the common object and the required connection between the offence and the object/knowledge must still be proved.

**How to use it:** Explain constructive liability under BNS sections 189–190. Do not claim that everyone present in a crowd is automatically liable.

### 7. Masalti v State of Uttar Pradesh
**Citation:** AIR 1965 SC 202  
**Syllabus connection:** Unlawful assembly; common object; proof of membership.

**Facts:** The case involved a serious group attack and the reliability of evidence identifying members of a large assembly.

**Issue:** How should a court evaluate evidence and liability where an offence is allegedly committed by a large unlawful assembly?

**Principle:** The Court discussed the evidentiary assessment in group offences and the application of unlawful-assembly liability. Group liability depends on proof of membership and statutory conditions; evidence must be assessed carefully.

**How to use it:** Use it to discuss why unlawful-assembly questions turn on reliable identification, membership, common object and the relation of the offence to that object.

**BNS caution:** Both *Lalji* and *Masalti* arose under earlier law. Start with BNS sections 189–190.

## C. Attempt and stages of crime

### 8. State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub
**Citation:** (1980) 3 SCC 57; AIR 1980 SC 1111  
**Syllabus connection:** Preparation versus attempt; BNS sections 62 and the relevant specific attempt offence.

**Facts:** The accused were moving silver towards a coastal location to unlawfully export it. Officials intercepted them near a place connected with the planned export.

**Issue:** Had the accused merely prepared for the offence, or had their conduct crossed the line into attempt?

**Principle:** Attempt cannot be reduced to a universal “last act” rule. The court examines intent and whether the act or series of acts has moved sufficiently beyond preparation and is reasonably proximate to the completed offence.

**How to use it:** In a problem, identify the planned offence, the act already done, what remained to be done and how close the conduct was to completion. Use the facts, not distance in time alone.

**BNS connection:** Use the principle with section 62 where it applies; check whether BNS provides a specific attempt offence.

### 9. Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar
**Citation:** AIR 1961 SC 1698  
**Syllabus connection:** Attempt; proximity; intention.

**Facts:** The case concerned an accused who used false representations and submitted materials in an effort to obtain permission to sit an examination. The issue was whether his conduct had passed beyond preparation.

**Issue:** Can acts performed before the final hoped-for result be an attempt?

**Principle:** An attempt does not require completion of the offence or performance of the final possible act. Conduct may amount to attempt when done with the requisite intent and when it has crossed the legally relevant boundary beyond preparation.

**How to use it:** This case is useful where a plan is partially executed but the target has not yet suffered the final consequence.

## D. Homicide and murder

### 10. State of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya
**Citation:** (1976) 4 SCC 382  
**Syllabus connection:** Culpable homicide versus murder; BNS sections 100–105.

**Facts:** The Supreme Court examined the distinction between culpable homicide and murder under the former IPC scheme.

**Issue:** How should a court determine whether culpable homicide falls within the narrower and more serious category of murder?

**Principle:** Culpable homicide is the broader category, and murder is a more serious species within it. A court should identify the relevant intention or knowledge, then ask whether the statutory definition of murder is met and whether an exception reduces the classification.

**How to use it:** Use this as the organising framework for an answer: (1) establish culpable homicide, (2) test the murder clauses, (3) examine exceptions, and (4) choose the correct punishment provision.

### 11. Virsa Singh v State of Punjab
**Citation:** AIR 1958 SC 465; 1958 SCR 1495  
**Syllabus connection:** Bodily injury and murder classification; BNS section 101.

**Facts:** The accused thrust a spear into the victim's abdomen. The injury caused death and medical evidence described it as sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of nature.

**Issue:** What must the prosecution prove where murder is alleged on the basis of an intentionally inflicted injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death?

**Principle:** Under the former IPC clause, the Court identified four key matters: an injury existed; its nature was proved; the accused intended to inflict that particular injury rather than inflicting it accidentally; and the injury was objectively sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death.

**How to use it:** Explain why the intention to inflict the particular injury and the objective sufficiency of that injury are distinct questions.

**BNS caution:** The case interpreted former IPC section 300's third clause. Check the corresponding wording of BNS section 101 before applying the exact four-step test.

### 12. K.M. Nanavati v State of Maharashtra
**Citation:** AIR 1962 SC 605; (1962) 2 SCR 567  
**Syllabus connection:** Grave and sudden provocation; murder exception under BNS section 101.

**Facts:** After learning of his wife's relationship with another man, Nanavati later confronted and killed the man. The case turned partly on whether the defence of grave and sudden provocation applied, including whether there had been time for passions to cool.

**Issue:** Does emotional distress automatically reduce murder to culpable homicide?

**Principle:** No. The statutory exception has conditions. The timing, nature of provocation, cooling-off period and accused's conduct must be assessed. A grave emotional reaction does not by itself prove the exception.

**How to use it:** In a provocation problem, discuss whether the provocation was legally sufficient, whether it was sudden, whether there was time to cool and what the accused did after the event.

### 13. Bachan Singh v State of Punjab
**Citation:** (1980) 2 SCC 684  
**Syllabus connection:** Punishment for murder; sentencing principles.

**Facts:** The case concerned the constitutional validity of the death penalty framework and principles governing capital sentencing.

**Issue:** Is death the automatic punishment when an offence is punishable with death or life imprisonment?

**Principle:** The Supreme Court upheld the death penalty framework while establishing the “rarest of rare” approach: life imprisonment is the rule and death is reserved for the exceptional category after proper sentencing analysis.

**How to use it:** Use it in a question about punishment after guilt is established, not to decide whether the accused committed murder in the first place.

## E. General Exceptions and defences

### 14. Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v State of Gujarat
**Citation:** AIR 1964 SC 1563  
**Syllabus connection:** Unsoundness of mind; BNS section 22.

**Facts:** The accused relied on insanity in a murder trial. The Court considered the legal test, the relevant time and the evidential burden for the defence.

**Issue:** How is legal insanity evaluated, and what is the effect if the defence is not conclusively established?

**Principle:** The legal question is the accused's capacity under the statutory test at the time of the act. The accused's evidential burden for an exception is distinct from the prosecution's burden to prove the offence. Evidence relied on for insanity can also raise reasonable doubt about a required ingredient of the offence.

**How to use it:** Do not write “medical illness equals legal insanity.” Explain the statutory incapacity and the evidence relevant to the time of the act.

**BNS caution:** This case arose under IPC section 84 and the former Evidence Act. Apply the principle through BNS section 22 and current evidence law.

### 15. Basdev v State of PEPSU
**Citation:** AIR 1956 SC 488  
**Syllabus connection:** Intoxication; intention and knowledge; BNS sections 23–24.

**Facts:** The accused was intoxicated during a shooting incident and claimed that intoxication affected his mental state.

**Issue:** Does voluntarily becoming intoxicated automatically remove criminal responsibility or the required mental element?

**Principle:** No. Voluntary intoxication is not a general defence. The court examines the applicable statutory rule and whether the particular intention/knowledge required by the offence is established on the evidence. The statutory treatment of involuntary intoxication differs.

**How to use it:** Identify whether intoxication was voluntary and what mental element the offence requires. Do not confuse the ability to form intention with a blanket immunity.

### 16. Darshan Singh v State of Punjab
**Citation:** (2010) 2 SCC 333  
**Syllabus connection:** Right of private defence; BNS sections 34–44.

**Facts:** The case examined a plea of private defence and the circumstances in which a person may use force to protect body or property.

**Issue:** What are the scope and limits of the right of private defence?

**Principle:** The right is preventive and protective, not punitive. It may arise where there is a reasonable apprehension of danger, and courts assess the circumstances as they appeared to the defender. The right has statutory limits and cannot be used as revenge after the danger has passed.

**How to use it:** Analyse the start and end of the right, the nature of the threat, reasonable apprehension, the act done in response, and whether the statutory limits were exceeded.

## F. Offences against property

### 17. K.N. Mehra v State of Rajasthan
**Citation:** AIR 1957 SC 369  
**Syllabus connection:** Theft; BNS section 303.

**Facts:** The case involved the taking and use of an aircraft without proper authority. The accused argued around the temporary nature and purpose of the taking.

**Issue:** Does temporary taking necessarily prevent the conduct from being theft?

**Principle:** The law focuses on the statutory ingredients, including dishonest taking/movement and wrongful gain/loss; temporary use does not by itself rule out theft. Intention is assessed from the circumstances and subsequent conduct may be evidence of that intention.

**How to use it:** When property was borrowed, used briefly or later returned, still analyse the mental element at the time of the taking and the definition of theft.

### 18. Pyare Lal Bhargava v State of Rajasthan
**Citation:** AIR 1963 SC 1094  
**Syllabus connection:** Theft; dishonest temporary removal; BNS section 303.

**Facts:** A government file was removed from an office temporarily and later returned. The removal was allegedly undertaken for an improper purpose.

**Issue:** Can temporary removal of property amount to theft even if the property is later returned?

**Principle:** Returning the property later does not automatically negate theft. The statutory dishonest intention and the act of moving property are assessed on the facts.

**How to use it:** The case is a strong example for rejecting the simplistic argument that “there is no theft because the thing was returned.”

### 19. R.K. Dalmia v Delhi Administration
**Citation:** AIR 1962 SC 1821  
**Syllabus connection:** Criminal breach of trust; meaning of property; BNS section 316.

**Facts:** The case concerned allegations involving funds and financial transactions in an organisational setting, and the meaning of “property” and criminal breach of trust under the former IPC scheme.

**Issue:** Can money/funds or property held through a company or other organisation be the subject of criminal breach of trust?

**Principle:** The Court interpreted the former statutory expression of property broadly in the relevant criminal-law context. For breach of trust, the key remains whether property or dominion was entrusted and then dealt with dishonestly in the prohibited way.

**How to use it:** State the two indispensable parts separately: entrustment/dominion and subsequent dishonest misappropriation, conversion, use or disposal. A financial disagreement alone is not enough.

### 20. Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar
**Citation:** (2000) 4 SCC 168  
**Syllabus connection:** Cheating versus breach of contract; BNS section 318.

**Facts:** The case arose from a disputed transaction in which a complainant alleged cheating after an arrangement was not performed as expected.

**Issue:** Does failure to honour an agreement automatically amount to cheating?

**Principle:** No. Cheating requires deception and the required dishonest/fraudulent inducement. The court examines whether the necessary dishonest intention existed at the relevant inception of the transaction; a later breach, without more, is not enough.

**How to use it:** Identify the representation, why it was false/deceptive, what the complainant did because of it, and what evidence shows the relevant intention at that time.

## G. Offences against women and children

### 21. State of Punjab v Gurmit Singh
**Citation:** (1996) 2 SCC 384  
**Syllabus connection:** Rape and sexual offences; evidence and assessment; BNS sections 63–79.

**Facts:** The Supreme Court considered principles governing the evaluation of the testimony of a rape survivor and the approach trial courts should take.

**Issue:** Is corroboration required as a rigid rule in every case of sexual assault?

**Principle:** The Court rejected a mechanical approach and stressed careful, sensitive assessment of the survivor's evidence. The testimony must be evaluated on the facts; the law does not impose a universal rule requiring corroboration in every case.

**How to use it:** Use the case to explain why courts must avoid stereotypes and rigid corroboration assumptions. It is a case about evidentiary approach, not a replacement for the statutory definition or proof of ingredients.

**BNS caution:** It was decided under the former IPC/Evidence Act framework. Apply the principle with the current BNS and BSA.

### 22. Satbir Singh v State of Haryana
**Citation:** (2021) 6 SCC 1  
**Syllabus connection:** Dowry death; BNS section 80.

**Facts:** The Supreme Court considered conviction for dowry death and how to understand the statutory phrase “soon before” death in the context of dowry-related cruelty or harassment.

**Issue:** Must the alleged dowry cruelty have happened immediately before the death?

**Principle:** “Soon before” does not mean “immediately before” in a fixed number of hours or days. There must be a live and proximate link between the dowry-related cruelty/harassment and the death, assessed on the facts; the statutory ingredients must all be met.

**How to use it:** Explain timing as a contextual legal requirement. Do not treat any past dowry dispute as automatically sufficient.

**BNS caution:** The case interpreted the former IPC dowry-death framework. Use it as a principle-based authority with BNS section 80 and current evidence law.

## H. How to use cases properly in an exam

### The four-sentence method
1. **Facts:** “In this case, the material facts were …”
2. **Issue:** “The Court had to decide whether …”
3. **Held/principle:** “The Court held that …”
4. **Application:** “This helps the present problem because …; however, the current offence is BNS section … and the exact statutory wording must be applied.”

### How many cases should I learn?
For a long answer, learn two or three leading cases for that topic well enough to explain their material facts and ratio. Do not attach a case name to every paragraph. A wrong or irrelevant case can weaken an otherwise correct answer.

## Sources and accuracy notes
Case citations are supplied so each decision can be located and checked. Summaries are educational paraphrases, not quotations from judgments. Case law under the IPC should be applied to the BNS only after checking whether the statutory language and legal issue remain materially comparable. BNS provisions such as section 103(2), section 152 and other new or materially changed provisions need their own current-text analysis; an older case cannot be represented as directly deciding those new provisions.
