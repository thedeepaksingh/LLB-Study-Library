# DAY-02 — Detailed Lesson: Liability and Inchoate Offences

**Study identifier:** DAY-02  
**Level:** Beginner  
**Language:** Simple English  
**Question status:** Questions at the end are predicted/practice questions, not verified previous-year questions.

## Learning outcomes

After this lesson, you should be able to explain common intention, common object, abetment, criminal conspiracy and attempt; identify the relevant BNS sections; and apply each rule to a hypothetical without assuming that group membership alone proves guilt.

## 1. Strict liability and constructive liability

### Strict liability

Strict liability refers to an offence or offence element for which the law does not require proof of the usual mental state in the same way as a conventional intention-based offence. Whether a mental element is required depends on the wording and interpretation of the particular provision.

Do not write that all BNS offences are strict-liability offences. Identify the exact provision and explain which element does or does not require proof of a mental state.

### Constructive or group liability

Constructive liability means the law may attribute an act or consequence to a person under a defined rule even though the person did not personally perform every physical act. Criminal group liability must be established through the applicable statute and facts. It cannot be assumed simply because two or more people were present.

## 2. Common intention — BNS Section 3(5)

Section 3(5) addresses a criminal act done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all. When the section applies, each person may be liable for that act in the manner stated by the provision.

When answering a problem, consider:
1. Who participated?
2. What common intention, if any, is supported by the evidence?
3. Was the act done in furtherance of that intention?
4. What conduct before, during and after the incident is relevant?
5. Are there facts inconsistent with a shared intention?

**Illustration:** A and B jointly plan to attack C and take part in carrying out the plan. Depending on the evidence and the legal ingredients, Section 3(5) may be relevant. The answer should not simply assume common intention because A and B were both present.

## 3. Unlawful assembly and common object — Sections 189 and 190

Section 189 defines unlawful assembly. In general terms, an unlawful assembly requires five or more persons and one of the common objects specified by the provision. Section 190 provides for liability of members for certain offences committed in prosecution of the common object, or which members knew to be likely, subject to its statutory conditions.

For a problem question:
1. Establish the number of persons and the statutory characteristics of an unlawful assembly.
2. Identify the common object.
3. Determine whether the accused was a member at the relevant time.
4. Examine the connection between the offence and the common object or the likelihood element in the provision.
5. Apply the complete statutory text and facts before reaching a conclusion.

### Common intention versus common object

| Point | Common intention | Common object |
|---|---|---|
| Main provision | Section 3(5) | Sections 189–190 |
| Core question | Was the act done in furtherance of the common intention of the participants? | Was there an unlawful assembly and are the conditions for member liability satisfied? |
| Number of persons | Section 3(5) itself does not impose a five-person minimum | Unlawful assembly generally requires five or more persons |
| Application | Examine shared intention and participation | Examine unlawful assembly, membership, common object and the statutory connection to the offence |

## 4. Abetment — Section 45 onward

Section 45 defines abetment of a thing. The statutory modes include instigation, conspiracy accompanied by an act or illegal omission in pursuance of that conspiracy, and intentional aid, subject to the precise language of the Act.

Use this structure:
- Identify the principal act or intended act.
- Identify the particular alleged mode of abetment.
- Explain the required mental element.
- Explain any act or omission required by that mode.
- Check the applicable punishment provision separately.

**Illustration:** A intentionally encourages B to commit an offence in circumstances that meet the statutory test for instigation. A may incur abetment liability even if A did not personally perform the principal act, depending on the statute and facts.

Do not say that every suggestion, association or conversation constitutes abetment. The legal ingredients must be proved.

## 5. Criminal conspiracy — Section 61

Section 61 concerns criminal conspiracy. Broadly, it focuses on an agreement between persons to do an unlawful act or a lawful act by unlawful means, subject to the statutory wording and qualifications.

In an exam answer:
- explain the agreement element;
- distinguish conspiracy from mere knowledge or presence;
- discuss the relevant statutory requirements for the nature of the agreement;
- explain any additional act required by the provision in the circumstances;
- identify the punishment rule only after checking the applicable subsection.

## 6. Attempt — Section 62 and specific provisions

Attempt concerns conduct that goes beyond mere preparation towards the commission of an offence, but the offence is not completed. Section 62 provides a general attempt rule for cases it covers where the Act does not separately provide for punishment of the attempt. Some offences have their own attempt provisions; attempt to murder is addressed specifically by Section 109.

### Intention, preparation, attempt and completion

- **Intention:** mental decision or purpose.
- **Preparation:** arranging means to commit the offence.
- **Attempt:** conduct crossing the legal threshold beyond mere preparation.
- **Completion:** all ingredients of the offence have been fulfilled.

Preparation is generally not punishable by itself, but some preparatory conduct is independently criminalised. Do not apply the four-stage framework mechanically; examine the provision and the conduct.

## 7. Exam-ready long-answer framework

**Question:** Distinguish common intention from common object under the BNS.

**Introduction:** Criminal law contains rules for attributing liability when several persons are involved in an offence. Common intention and common object are separate legal concepts with different statutory requirements.

**Common intention:** Section 3(5) applies to a criminal act done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all. The answer should explain the shared intention and the connection between that intention and the act.

**Common object:** Sections 189 and 190 concern unlawful assembly and liability for specified offences connected to the assembly's common object or known likelihood. The answer should address the minimum number of persons and all statutory conditions.

**Distinction:** Common intention is not the same as common object. Common-object liability is tied to the unlawful-assembly provisions. The facts must be assessed under the correct rule.

**Conclusion:** The prosecution must establish the relevant statutory ingredients. Mere presence or association should not replace legal analysis.
