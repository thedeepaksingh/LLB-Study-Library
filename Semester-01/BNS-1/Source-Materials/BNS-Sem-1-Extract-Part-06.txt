VI. EXTORTION

🔴 SECTION 308 – EXTORTION
This is one of the most important provisions in the entire chapter.

The BNS consolidates the old IPC extortion provisions into §308 with multiple sub-sections.




Definition
A person commits extortion when he:

      1. intentionally puts or attempts to put another person in fear of injury;
      2. does so to that person or another;
      3. thereby dishonestly induces the person put in fear;
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 385 / 748>
    4. to deliver property, valuable security or signed/sealed thing capable of becoming
       valuable security.

Simple formula

        Fear of injury → Dishonest inducement → Delivery of property/security




Example
A tells B:

        "Give me ₹50,000 or I will burn your shop."

→ Extortion.




⭐ Essential ingredients
1. Intention
The offender must intentionally create fear.

2. Fear of injury
"Injury" is broader than merely physical injury.

3. Dishonest inducement
The fear must cause the victim to deliver something.

4. Delivery
The victim must be induced to deliver:

    ●    property;
    ●    valuable security; or
    ●    signed/sealed thing capable of becoming valuable security.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 386 / 748>

     ●    injury;
     ●    death;
     ●    grievous hurt;

A suddenly grabs B's mobile phone from B's hand.

Answer: Snatching under §304, because the theft is committed by sudden/quick/forcible
seizure from the person/possession.




Problem 3
A points a gun at B and demands B's wallet. B immediately hands it over.

Answer: Robbery under §309, because extortion becomes robbery where the required
immediate fear and presence conditions are satisfied.




Problem 4
Five persons jointly commit the above robbery.

Answer: Dacoity under §310, provided all five conjointly participate in committing/attempting
the robbery.




Problem 5
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 413 / 748>
A is entrusted with ₹10 lakh to purchase machinery. He dishonestly uses the money to
purchase a personal car.

Answer: Criminal breach of trust under §316, because entrustment followed by dishonest
conversion/use is present.




Problem 6
A finds B's lost wallet and later decides to keep it dishonestly.

Answer: Criminal misappropriation under §314, subject to the statutory requirements.




Problem 7
A takes an advance from B after falsely claiming that he possesses goods for immediate
delivery, although A knows he has no intention of supplying them.

Answer: Cheating under §318, because the dishonest deception/inducement existed at the
relevant initial stage.




Problem 8
A enters B's property intending to intimidate B.

Answer: Criminal trespass under §329, assuming the statutory elements are established.

   SECTION 335 – MAKING A FALSE

This is the foundation of the entire forgery chapter.

The BNS expressly includes electronic records and electronic signatures, which is
particularly important in modern criminal law.

🔄 IPC → BNS
IPC §464 → BNS §335




⭐ What is a false document?
A person makes a false document/electronic record when, dishonestly or fraudulently, he falls
within one of the statutory categories.

There are essentially three situations.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 418 / 748>
1. Making a document in another person's name/authority
A person:

      ●    makes;
      ●    signs;
      ●    seals;
      ●    executes

a document, or

      ●    makes/transmits an electronic record;
      ●    affixes an electronic signature;
      ●    makes a mark representing execution/authenticity,

with the intention that it be believed that it was made or authorised by another person when he
knows that it was not.

Example
A signs B's name on a sale agreement without B's authority.

→ False document.




2. Material alteration
A person, without lawful authority:

      ●    dishonestly or fraudulently;
      ●    alters a document or electronic record;
      ●    in a material part.

Example
A changes:

          ₹50,000

to:

          ₹5,00,000

in a signed agreement without authority.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 419 / 748>
→ False document.




3. Causing another person to execute a
document by deception/incapacity
A causes another person to:

   ●   sign;
   ●   seal;
   ●   execute;
   ●   alter a document/electronic record; or
   ●   affix an electronic signature,

while knowing that the person:

   ●   cannot understand it because of unsoundness of mind/intoxication; or
   ●   does not know its contents because of deception.




⭐ Three-part memory
335 = MAKE

   1. Pretend to be another
   2. Materially alter
   3. Cause another to sign/alter through deception or incapacity




🔴 SECTION 336 – FORGERY
Forgery is based upon making a false document with the required fraudulent/dishonest
purpose.

🔄 IPC → BNS
Broadly corresponds to the IPC framework of:

   ●   §463 – forgery definition
   ●   §465 – punishment
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 420 / 748>
   ●   §468 – forgery for cheating
   ●   §469 – forgery for harming reputation.




Definition
A person commits forgery if he:

   1. makes a false document/electronic record;
   2. with the intention of:
          ○ causing damage or injury;
          ○ supporting a claim/title;
          ○ causing a person to part with property;
          ○ entering into an express or implied contract; or
          ○ committing fraud.




⭐forgery
    Essential ingredients of

1. Making a false document
This is essential.

2. Fraudulent/dishonest intention
There must be the required criminal intention.

3. Statutory purpose
The false document must be made for one of the purposes specified in the section.




⚖️ Important case law
Mohd. Ibrahim v State of Bihar
The Supreme Court explained an important principle concerning making a false document.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 421 / 748>
A person does not automatically commit forgery merely because he executes a document
concerning property to which he may not be entitled.

The question is whether the statutory requirements for making a false document are actually
satisfied.

🔴 Exam principle:
         Always establish the making of a "false document" before concluding that forgery
         has been committed.

Up to:

   ●      7 years, and
   ●      fine.

PART I – CRIMINAL INTIMIDATION

🔴INTIMIDATION
    SECTION 351 – CRIMINAL

🔄 IPC → BNS
IPC §503 → BNS §351(1)
 IPC §506 → BNS §351(2)

Criminal intimidation is essentially:

         Threat + intention to cause alarm / compel conduct




⭐ Definition
A person commits criminal intimidation when he threatens another with:

     ●    injury to person;
     ●    injury to reputation;
     ●    injury to property; or
     ●    injury to the person or reputation of someone in whom that person is interested,

with the intention to:

Either
A. cause alarm to that person

OR

B. cause that person to do something which he is not legally bound to do

OR

C. cause that person to omit something which he is legally entitled to do

where such act/omission is intended to avoid execution of the threat.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 443 / 748>
⭐ Simple formula
      THREAT + INTENTION + ALARM/COMPEL = CRIMINAL INTIMIDATION




Example 1 – Threat to person
A tells B:

      "If you testify against me, I will kill you."

If the statutory requirements are established:

→ Criminal intimidation under §351.




Example 2 – Threat to reputation
A tells B:

      "Give me ₹1 lakh or I will publish false allegations about you."

→ Threat to reputation + intended coercion.

This can constitute criminal intimidation, subject to the statutory requirements.




Example 3 – Threat to property
A tells B:

      "Do not file the case or I will burn your shop."

→ Threat to property.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 444 / 748>
🔴necessary
    Important: Actual physical harm is NOT

The offence is based on the threat and required intention.

The offender does not necessarily have to actually carry out the threat.

⭐ Exam point
       Criminal intimidation is complete when the statutory ingredients of threatening
       conduct and the requisite intention are established; actual execution of the threat is
       not necessary.




⭐ What can be threatened?
Remember:

P-R-P
P – Person
R – Reputation
P – Property

And also:

       Person/reputation of someone in whom the victim is interested.




Punishment
Under §351, ordinary criminal intimidation is punishable with:

   ●    imprisonment up to 2 years, or
   ●    fine, or
   ●    both.

🔴 Aggravated intimidation
Where the threat is to cause:

   ●    death;
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 445 / 748>
   ●   grievous hurt;
   ●   destruction of property by fire;
   ●   an offence punishable with death/life imprisonment/imprisonment up to 7 years;
   ●   imputing unchastity to a woman,

the punishment may extend to:

   ●   7 years, or
   ●   fine, or
   ●   both.




⭐ Anonymous threats
The provision also deals with threats communicated anonymously or where the offender
conceals his identity/address.

Additional punishment
There can be an additional punishment of:

   ●   imprisonment up to 2 years,

in addition to the punishment for criminal intimidation.

The Supreme Court discussed the ingredients of criminal intimidation and emphasised that the
threat must fall within the statutory requirements.

A mere expression of anger or criticism does not automatically constitute criminal intimidation.

🔄 IPC → BNS

   ●    communal relations.




⭐ Key idea
The offence is concerned not merely with whether a statement is false, but with its potential

Important categories
The provision covers statements/reports/rumours which may:

1. Cause members of the armed forces to mutiny or disregard duty

2. Cause fear/alarm to members of the public

Where such fear may induce a person to commit an offence against the State or against public

The provision must be read together with the relevant statutory exceptions and explanations.

BNS §196
Promoting enmity between groups.

BNS §353
Statements/rumours/reports producing the specified public-mischief consequences.

PART VI – DEFAMATION

🔴 SECTION 356 – DEFAMATION
This is one of the most important provisions of Chapter XIX.

🔄 IPC → BNS
IPC §499 → BNS §356(1)
 IPC §500 → BNS §356(2)




⭐ Definition
A person commits defamation when he:

   ●   makes or publishes an imputation concerning any person;
   ●   intending to harm, or knowing/reasonably believing it will harm, that person's reputation.

The imputation may be made through:

   ●   words;
   ●   signs;
   ●   visible representations;
   ●   electronic means.




⭐ Formula
       IMPUTATION + PUBLICATION + REPUTATION + REQUIRED
       INTENTION/KNOWLEDGE = DEFAMATION
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 453 / 748>
🔴 The four key concepts
1. Imputation
There must be an assertion/imputation concerning a person.

It can relate to:

    ●   conduct;
    ●   character;
    ●   profession;
    ●   actions;
    ●   other matters affecting reputation.




2. Concerning a person
The imputation must relate to an identifiable person.

That person may be:

    ●   an individual;
    ●   a company;
    ●   an association;
    ●   collection of persons,

subject to the statutory requirements.




3. Intention/knowledge
The accused must:

    ●   intend to harm reputation; OR
    ●   know that the imputation will harm reputation; OR
    ●   have reason to believe it will harm reputation.




4. Reputation
The imputation must be legally capable of harming the person's reputation.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 454 / 748>
⭐ What is reputation?
The law protects reputation as a social estimation of a person.

The relevant question is not simply:

      "Did the person feel insulted?"

The question is whether the imputation is capable of lowering the person's reputation in the
legally relevant sense.




⚖️ Important case law
Subramanian Swamy v Union of India
The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of criminal defamation.

Exam principle
The right to reputation is recognised as an important aspect of the protection of individual
dignity, while freedom of speech under Article 19(1)(a) is subject to constitutionally permitted
restrictions.
