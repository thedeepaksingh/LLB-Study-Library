🔴essential
    Q1. What is a crime? Explain its
           elements and stages of crime.
Answer
Introduction
Crime is an act or omission prohibited by criminal law and punishable by the State. Criminal law
protects society by identifying conduct considered sufficiently harmful to justify public
punishment.

A crime is not merely a morally wrong act. It must constitute conduct prohibited by law.

The basic principle of criminal liability can be expressed as:

Actus Reus + Mens Rea + Causation, where required + absence of defence = Criminal
liability




I. Essential elements of crime
1. Human conduct
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 623 / 748>
Criminal liability ordinarily arises from a person's voluntary conduct.

Conduct may consist of:

   ●   an act; or
   ●   an omission where the law imposes a duty to act.

Mere thoughts or intentions are generally not punishable.

Example:
A thinks about killing B but takes no action. Mere intention does not ordinarily constitute
murder.




2. Actus Reus
Actus reus means the physical element of the offence.

It may include:

   ●   a prohibited act;
   ●   an unlawful omission;
   ●   a prohibited consequence;
   ●   specified circumstances.




3. Mens Rea
Mens rea means the guilty mental element.

Depending upon the offence, it may be:

   ●   intention;
   ●   knowledge;
   ●   dishonesty;
   ●   fraudulence;
   ●   reason to believe;
   ●   other mental states specified by statute.




4. Concurrence
The required mental element must ordinarily accompany the prohibited conduct.




5. Causation
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 624 / 748>
Where an offence requires a particular result, the prosecution must establish that the accused's
conduct caused that result.

For example, in homicide, the prosecution must prove the causal connection between the
accused's conduct and death.




6. Legality
Criminal liability must be based upon law.

The principle is:

Nullum crimen sine lege – there is no crime without law.




II. Stages of crime
Crime generally proceeds through four stages:

1. Intention
The person forms the intention to commit an offence.

Generally not punishable.

2. Preparation
The person arranges the means necessary to commit the offence.

Generally not punishable unless specifically criminalised.

3. Attempt
The person performs an act sufficiently proximate to the commission of the offence.

BNS §62 deals with general attempt liability.

4. Commission
The offence is completed.




⚖️ Important cases
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 625 / 748>
Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar – distinction between preparation and attempt.

State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub – proximity between the act and intended offence.




⭐ Exam conclusion
The law generally does not punish mere criminal thoughts. Liability develops as conduct moves
from intention to preparation, attempt and finally commission, subject to specific statutory
offences.

     Q2. Explain Mens Rea and the principle
             responsibility.
Answer
Introduction
Mens rea means guilty mind. It is the mental element required for criminal liability.

The classical principle is:

Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea

An act does not ordinarily make a person guilty unless accompanied by a guilty mind.




Forms of mens rea
1. Intention
Intention means the conscious objective to produce a particular result.

Example: A shoots B intending to kill him.

2. Knowledge
Knowledge means awareness of circumstances or consequences relevant to the offence.

3. Dishonesty
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 626 / 748>
Dishonesty is particularly important in offences relating to property.

4. Fraudulence
Fraudulent intention is relevant to offences involving deception and fraudulent conduct.

5. Reason to believe
Certain offences use this statutory standard rather than direct knowledge.




Importance of mens rea
Mens rea:

   ●   protects persons from punishment for genuinely innocent conduct;
   ●   distinguishes accident from intentional crime;
   ●   determines seriousness of criminal conduct;
   ●   helps courts interpret statutory offences.




Mens rea and statutory offences
Mens rea is not mechanically required in identical form for every offence.

The court must examine the language and ingredients of the particular statute.

Some offences may impose liability substantially on proof of prohibited conduct without requiring
the same traditional mental element.




Mens rea under BNS
The BNS repeatedly uses mental-state terms such as:

   ●   intentionally;
   ●   knowingly;
   ●   voluntarily;
   ●   dishonestly;
   ●   fraudulently;
   ●   reason to believe.

Therefore, while the general doctrine remains important, the specific statutory wording must
always be examined.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 627 / 748>
⭐ Exam conclusion
Mens rea is one of the fundamental foundations of criminal responsibility. However, its precise
form depends upon the particular offence.

   Q3. Explain the General Exceptions
       the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita.
Answer
Introduction
The BNS recognises circumstances in which conduct that would otherwise constitute an offence
is not criminally punishable.

The General Exceptions are principally contained in BNS §§14–44.

These exceptions recognise that criminal liability should not arise where the required conditions
for responsibility are absent or where the law justifies the conduct.




Important General Exceptions
1. Act done under legal obligation – §14
A person may be protected where the act is done under a legal obligation or under a mistake of
fact concerning such obligation.




2. Judicial acts – §15
Acts done by a judge acting judicially within the relevant legal protection are covered.




3. Acts done pursuant to court order – §16
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 628 / 748>
A person acting pursuant to a judgment or order of a competent court may receive statutory
protection.




4. Mistake of fact – §17
A person may be protected where the relevant mistake concerns fact rather than law and
statutory conditions are satisfied.

Mistake of law ordinarily does not excuse.




5. Accident – §18
Accidental harm arising during lawful conduct performed lawfully and with proper care may fall
within the exception.




6. Necessity – §19
Conduct may be protected where done to prevent greater harm in circumstances recognised by
law.




7. Children – §§20–21
   ●   Under seven: absolute protection.
   ●   Above seven and under twelve: protection where sufficient maturity of understanding is
       absent.




8. Unsoundness of mind – §22
Protection applies where, because of unsoundness of mind, the person was incapable of
understanding the nature of the act or that it was wrong/contrary to law, as required by the
statute.




9. Intoxication – §§23–24
The BNS distinguishes involuntary intoxication from voluntary intoxication.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 629 / 748>
10. Consent – §§25–31
Certain acts involving consent are protected where the statutory requirements are satisfied.




11. Compulsion by threats – §32
Specified acts done under threats may receive protection subject to statutory limitations.




12. Slight harm – §33
Acts causing slight harm may fall within the statutory exception.




13. Private defence – §§34–44
The law permits protection of:

   ●   body;
   ●   another person's body;
   ●   property;
   ●   another person's property,

subject to statutory conditions and limitations.




⚖️ Important cases
Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v State of Gujarat – insanity.

Darshan Singh v State of Punjab – private defence.

James Martin v State of Kerala – scope of private defence.




⭐ Exam conclusion
General Exceptions are essential because criminal law does not impose liability mechanically.
The court must first determine whether the prosecution has established the offence and then
consider whether any applicable statutory exception protects the accused.

   Q4. Explain the right of private defence
       BNS §§34–44.
Answer
Introduction
The right of private defence is a fundamental criminal-law defence. It recognises the right of a
person to protect oneself and others against unlawful aggression.

The relevant provisions are BNS §§34–44.




Objects of private defence
The right may protect:

Body
A person may defend:

   ●   his own body;
   ●   the body of another person.

Property
A person may defend:

   ●   his own property;
   ●   property of another person.




Essential conditions
1. There must be apprehension of danger
The right arises when circumstances create the legally relevant apprehension of harm.

2. The threat must be legally recognised
The danger must arise from conduct covered by the statutory provisions.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 631 / 748>
3. The right is preventive
Private defence is intended to prevent harm.

It is not a licence for revenge.

4. Force must remain within statutory limits
The amount of force must be examined in the circumstances.

5. Right continues only while the danger exists
Once the threat has ceased, continued retaliation cannot ordinarily be justified as private
defence.




When private defence may extend to causing death
The BNS specifies serious circumstances in which the right of private defence of the body may
extend to causing death.

Similarly, specified serious offences against property may justify the more extensive defensive
response permitted by the statute.




Limitations
The right does not ordinarily exist:

    ●   where there is no reasonable apprehension;
    ●   where there is sufficient opportunity to seek public authorities in circumstances excluded
        by law;
    ●   for revenge;
    ●   to inflict unlimited harm.




⚖️ Cases
Darshan Singh v State of Punjab
The Supreme Court explained that the right of private defence should be assessed realistically
from the circumstances confronting the accused.

James Martin v State of Kerala
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 632 / 748>
The Court emphasised that the accused need not measure defensive force with mathematical
precision.




⭐ Important principle
Private defence is a shield, not a sword.

Culpable homicide becomes murder when it falls within one of the statutory clauses and none of

Four clauses of murder
Clause 1 – Intention to cause death
Where the accused intends to cause death.

Clause 2 – Particular bodily injury
Where the accused intends to cause bodily injury knowing that it is likely to cause death of that

Where the accused intentionally causes bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature

   3.   Public servant exceeding powers.
