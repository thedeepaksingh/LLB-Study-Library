An imputation of truth is not defamation when:

   1. it is true; and
   2. its publication is for the public good.

⚠️ Both aspects matter.

CHAPTER XX – REPEAL AND SAVINGS
BNS §358
🔴 SHORT CHAPTER — BUT MUST KNOW
This is the final substantive provision of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

Unlike the previous chapters, there are no major offences to memorise here. The importance is
legal transition from IPC, 1860 to BNS, 2023.




SECTION 358 – REPEAL AND SAVINGS
🔄 IPC → BNS
Indian Penal Code, 1860 → Repealed by BNS §358

Section 358 provides that the Indian Penal Code, 1860 is repealed.

However, the repeal is subject to important savings clauses.




I. WHAT DOES "REPEAL" MEAN?
Repeal means:

      The earlier law is formally revoked or taken off the statute book.

Therefore, after BNS came into force:

IPC → repealed

BNS → principal substantive criminal law

BNS came into force on 1 July 2024, except §106(2), which was excluded from the
commencement notification.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 468 / 748>
II. WHY IS A SAVINGS CLAUSE
NECESSARY?
Suppose:

   ●    an offence occurred under the IPC before BNS commenced;
   ●    an investigation was already pending;
   ●    a trial was already going on;
   ●    a conviction had already occurred;
   ●    an appeal was pending.

If repeal simply erased everything connected with the IPC, enormous legal uncertainty would
result.

Therefore:

       Repeal does not mean that all previous legal consequences disappear.

The savings clause preserves specified:

   ●    previous operations;
   ●    rights;
   ●    liabilities;
   ●    penalties;
   ●    investigations;
   ●    proceedings;
   ●    remedies.




⭐ CORE PRINCIPLE
Remember:

       REPEAL ≠ ERASURE OF PAST LEGAL CONSEQUENCES

The repeal of IPC does not ordinarily make an act that was criminal when committed
retrospectively lawful.

This is particularly important because of Article 20(1) of the Constitution.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 469 / 748>
III. ARTICLE 20(1) – IMPORTANT
CONSTITUTIONAL CONNECTION
Article 20(1) provides protection against:

Ex post facto criminal laws.
A person cannot be:

   ●   convicted for an act which was not an offence when it was committed; or
   ●   subjected to a greater penalty than what was applicable when the offence was
       committed.

⭐ Example
Suppose:

1 January 2024:
A commits an offence under IPC.

1 July 2024:
BNS comes into force.

A cannot simply be prosecuted under BNS merely because BNS is the law currently in force if
doing so would violate Article 20(1).

The law applicable to the offence at the relevant time remains constitutionally significant.




IV. SAVING PENDING INVESTIGATIONS
AND PROCEEDINGS
Section 358 preserves the operation of the repealed IPC for specified matters arising before
repeal.

This includes, subject to the statutory wording:

   ●   investigations;
   ●   inquiries;
   ●   proceedings;
   ●   appeals;
   ●   rights;
   ●   liabilities;
   ●   penalties;
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 470 / 748>
      ●   punishments.

⭐ Exam principle
If an offence was committed before BNS commenced, the repeal of IPC does not
automatically terminate the criminal process relating to that offence.




V. EXAMPLE – OFFENCE BEFORE BNS
Suppose:

20 June 2024
A commits an offence corresponding to IPC §379 (theft).

1 July 2024
BNS comes into force.

Question
Does the prosecution disappear because IPC has been repealed?

Answer
No.

The repeal-and-savings framework preserves the legal consequences of the earlier offence.

The prosecution can continue under the applicable transitional legal framework.

Section 358 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 repeals the Indian Penal Code, 1860.

🔴essential
    Q1. What is Crime? Explain the
           elements of crime.
Answer
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 502 / 748>
Introduction
A crime is an act or omission prohibited by law and made punishable by the State.

Unlike a purely private wrong, a crime is regarded as an offence against society and the State.




Essential elements
1. Human act or omission
There must ordinarily be an act or legally punishable omission.

2. Mens rea
There must generally be a guilty mind such as:

   ●    intention;
   ●    knowledge;
   ●    recklessness;
   ●    dishonesty;
   ●    negligence,

depending upon the particular offence.

3. Actus reus
The prohibited physical act or omission must be established.

4. Legally prohibited conduct
The conduct must constitute an offence under law.

5. Punishment
The law must prescribe a criminal consequence.




⭐ Maxim
       Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea

An act does not make a person criminally liable unless accompanied by a guilty mind, subject to
statutory exceptions such as strict liability offences.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 503 / 748>
Conclusion
Thus, crime generally involves:

       Actus Reus + Mens Rea + Prohibited Conduct + Punishment




🔴importance
    Q2. Explain Mens Rea and its
            in criminal law.
Answer
Mens rea means the guilty mind or criminal mental element accompanying the prohibited
conduct.

It may take the form of:

   ●    intention;
   ●    knowledge;
   ●    dishonesty;
   ●    fraud;
   ●    recklessness;
   ●    negligence,

depending upon the offence.

Importance
Mens rea distinguishes:

   ●    accidental conduct from intentional crime;
   ●    innocent conduct from criminal conduct;
   ●    ordinary negligence from criminal negligence.

⚠️ Exception
Mens rea is not an absolute requirement for every statutory offence. Certain offences may
impose liability on the basis prescribed by statute.

⭐ Exam line
       Mens rea is a fundamental principle of criminal responsibility, but its application
       depends upon the wording, nature and object of the particular offence.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 504 / 748>
🔴commission
   Q3. Explain the stages in the
            of a crime.
Answer
There are generally four stages:


1. Intention
The first stage is formation of criminal intention.

Mere intention ordinarily is not punishable.




2. Preparation
Preparation means arranging the means necessary to commit the offence.

Example
A decides to rob a bank and obtains a weapon for that purpose.

Ordinarily, preparation itself is not punishable unless the law specifically makes it an offence.




3. Attempt
Attempt begins when the accused performs an act sufficiently proximate to the commission of
the offence.

Under BNS §62, attempt to commit an offence punishable under the Sanhita, where no specific
attempt provision exists, is punishable.




4. Commission
The final stage is completion of the offence.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 505 / 748>
Formula

      Intention → Preparation → Attempt → Commission

  ●    Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar
  ●    Malkiat Singh v State of Punjab
  ●    State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub
