PART I — INTRODUCTION TO BNS
1. What is BNS?
Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) is India's principal substantive criminal law dealing with
offences and punishments.

It replaced the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC) from 1 July 2024. The official statute describes
BNS as an Act to consolidate and amend provisions relating to offences and matters connected
with or incidental to them.

Three new criminal laws
You should immediately remember this distinction:


     Law               Replaced                          Subject


 BNS, 2023     IPC, 1860                    Substantive criminal law / offences


 BNSS,         CrPC, 1973                   Criminal procedure
 2023


 BSA, 2023     Indian Evidence Act, 1872    Law of evidence


The official India Code records BNS as Act 45 of 2023, BNSS as Act 46, and BSA as Act 47.

⭐ Remember
BNS = What is a crime + punishment
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 4 / 748>
BNSS = How a criminal case proceeds

BSA = How facts are proved

This distinction is extremely important in LLB.

CHAPTER I – PRELIMINARY


SECTION 1 – SHORT TITLE,
COMMENCEMENT AND APPLICATION
BNS Section: 1
Corresponding IPC Provision: IPC Section 1

🔴 MUST STUDY
Section 1 deals with three basic matters:

   1. Short title of the law
   2. Commencement of the law
   3. Territorial and extra-territorial application of the law




1. Short Title
The Act is called the:

“Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023”

It may be abbreviated as BNS.

The BNS replaced the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC) as the principal substantive criminal law
of India.




2. Commencement of BNS
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 12 / 748>
BNS came into force on 1 July 2024, except the provision relating to Section 106(2), which was
excluded from the commencement notification.

⭐ EXAM POINT
For examination purposes, remember:

       BNS came into force on 1 July 2024.

Therefore, offences committed after the commencement of BNS are ordinarily dealt with under
BNS, subject to the rules governing the applicability of criminal law to acts committed before the
change in law.




3. Territorial Application
BNS generally applies to a person who commits an act or omission contrary to its provisions
within India.

This reflects the basic principle of territorial jurisdiction in criminal law.

Example
A commits an offence in Delhi.

Since the offence is committed within India, BNS applies, subject to the particular facts and
other applicable laws.




4. Extra-Territorial Application
BNS also contains provisions dealing with certain acts committed outside India.

This is important because criminal jurisdiction is not always restricted to offences physically
committed within Indian territory.

Section 1 extends the application of BNS to certain persons and situations outside India,
including:

   ●    An Indian citizen committing an offence outside India;
   ●    A person on a ship or aircraft registered in India, wherever it may be;
   ●    A person outside India who commits an offence targeting a computer resource located in
        India.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 13 / 748>
5. Offence Outside India by an Indian Citizen
Where an Indian citizen commits an offence outside India, BNS may apply to that conduct.

Example
A, an Indian citizen, commits an offence in another country.

The fact that the offence occurred outside Indian territory does not automatically prevent BNS
from applying.




6. Offence on Indian-Registered Ship or
Aircraft
BNS may also apply to offences committed by a person on:

   ●   a ship registered in India, or
   ●   an aircraft registered in India,

regardless of where the ship or aircraft is located.

Example
A commits an offence on an aircraft registered in India while the aircraft is outside Indian
territory.

The extra-territorial application provisions of BNS may make the offender subject to BNS.




7. Offence Targeting a Computer Resource
in India
This is one of the significant modern aspects of BNS.

BNS extends to a person outside India who commits an offence targeting a computer resource
located in India.

Example
A person sitting outside India unlawfully attacks a computer system located in India.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 14 / 748>
The fact that the offender is physically outside India does not necessarily prevent application of
BNS.

⭐ EXAM POINT
This reflects the increasing importance of cybercrime and digital jurisdiction.

SECTION 1 – EXAM ANSWER
Question:
Explain the extent and application of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

Answer:
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 15 / 748>
Section 1 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 deals with the short title, commencement and
application of the Sanhita.

The Act is called the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and came into force on 1 July 2024,
subject to the notified exception concerning Section 106(2).

The Sanhita generally applies to offences committed within India. It also has extra-territorial
application in specified circumstances. It may apply to an Indian citizen who commits an offence
outside India, to offences committed on a ship or aircraft registered in India, and to certain
offences committed outside India which target a computer resource located in India.

Thus, Section 1 establishes both the territorial and specified extra-territorial reach of BNS.

2.1 “Act”
The term act includes a series of acts as well as a single act.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 17 / 748>
Therefore, an offence need not always consist of one isolated physical action.

Example
A performs several connected acts which together constitute the commission of an offence.

The law may treat the series of acts collectively as an act.

⭐ Remember:
       Act includes a series of acts.




2.2 “Child”
Under BNS, a child means a person below the age of 18 years.

⭐ EXAM POINT
       Child = person below 18 years

This definition is particularly relevant to offences involving children.




2.3 “Dishonestly”
A person is said to do something dishonestly when he does it with the intention of causing:

   ●    wrongful gain to one person, or
   ●    wrongful loss to another person.

Formula
Dishonestly = Intention to cause wrongful gain OR wrongful loss

Example
A takes B's property intending to obtain an unlawful benefit for himself.

The act may be characterised as being done dishonestly.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 18 / 748>
2.4 “Fraudulently”
A person is said to do something fraudulently if he does it with the intention of defrauding.

Important distinction
Do not confuse:

Dishonestly
with
Fraudulently

               Dishonestly                          Fraudulently

 Wrongful gain/loss                        Intention to defraud

 Focus on gain or loss                     Focus on fraud

 Frequently relevant to property           Relevant to fraudulent conduct
 offences




2.5 “Document”
The concept of document is extremely important in modern criminal law.

It includes a matter expressed or described upon any substance by means of:

   ●   letters,
   ●   figures,
   ●   marks,
   ●   or more than one of these means,

which is intended to be used as evidence of that matter.

The definition also accommodates modern forms of documentation, including electronic and
digital records.

⚠️ BNS MODERNISATION
This is important because criminal offences involving documents now operate in an environment
where evidence may exist digitally.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 19 / 748>
2.6 “Good Faith”
An act is not done or believed to be done in good faith merely because it is honestly done.

The law requires due care and attention.

⭐ Very Important
       Good faith requires due care and attention.

Therefore:

Honesty alone ≠ Good faith

Example
A doctor honestly believes a particular treatment is appropriate but completely ignores obvious
precautions that a reasonable professional should take.

The mere fact that the doctor acted honestly does not automatically establish good faith.




2.7 “Movable Property”
Movable property includes property of every description except land and things attached to
the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth.

This definition becomes important in offences such as:

   ●    theft,
   ●    robbery,
   ●    misappropriation,
   ●    criminal breach of trust.




2.8 “Person”
The term person includes:

   ●    a company,
   ●    an association,
   ●    or body of persons,
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 20 / 748>
whether incorporated or not.

⭐ Exam relevance
Criminal law is therefore not restricted to natural human beings for every purpose.




2.9 “Public Servant”
BNS provides an extensive definition of public servant.

The concept covers persons holding various public offices or performing specified public
functions.

This becomes particularly relevant to offences:

   ●   committed by public servants,
   ●   involving public servants,
   ●   involving public authority.




2.10 “Voluntarily”
A person is said to cause an effect voluntarily when he causes it by means whereby he
intended to cause it, or by means which he knew or had reason to believe were likely to cause
it.

Example
A intentionally strikes B with a weapon knowing that the blow is likely to cause serious injury.

If the relevant consequence occurs, the concept of voluntarily causing that consequence
becomes relevant.




2.11 “Wrongfully”
A person is said to do something wrongfully when he does it with the intention of causing
wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another, or causes such a result by
unlawful means.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 21 / 748>
The precise statutory wording should be learned from the provision when preparing a final
examination answer.

SECTION 3 – GENERAL EXPLANATIONS
BNS Section: 3
Corresponding IPC: Mainly corresponding to the General Explanations in IPC Sections 6–
52

🔴 MUST STUDY
Section 3 contains general explanations that apply to the interpretation and operation of BNS.

These explanations are important because they prevent the need to repeat the same principles
in every individual offence.




3.1 General Explanations Apply Throughout BNS
Unless the context indicates otherwise, the general explanations contained in BNS apply
throughout the Sanhita.

This is an important principle of statutory interpretation.




3.2 Acts and Omissions
Criminal liability may arise not only from a positive act, but in appropriate circumstances from an
omission where the law imposes a duty to act.

Example
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 23 / 748>
A person who has a legal duty to provide necessary care deliberately fails to perform that duty,
resulting in the legally relevant consequence.

The omission may therefore have criminal consequences where the relevant offence and legal
duty are established.




3.3 Common Intention / Joint Liability
Where a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all,
each person may be liable as if the act had been done by him alone.

⭐ Very Important
This is one of the most frequently tested principles in criminal law.

IPC Reference

IPC §34 → BNS §3(5)

Example
A and B jointly attack C pursuant to a common intention to cause his death.

If the prosecution establishes the required common intention and participation, both may be
held liable for the resulting act.




3.4 Important Principle
Mere presence at the scene of an offence does not automatically establish common
intention.

There must be sufficient evidence showing:

   1. Participation;
   2. Common intention; and
   3. Criminal act done in furtherance of that common intention.




⚖️ CASE LAW — COMMON INTENTION
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 24 / 748>
Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King-Emperor
The Supreme Court/Privy Council-era principle associated with common intention establishes
that a person may be liable even though he did not personally perform the physical act
constituting the offence, provided the requirements of joint liability/common intention are
established.

Mahbub Shah v. Emperor
The case is important for understanding that common intention requires a meeting of minds,
and it cannot simply be presumed from the fact that several persons were present.

Quote these cases when answering:

1. SECTION 4 – PUNISHMENTS
BNS Section: 4
Corresponding IPC: IPC §53


🔴 MUST STUDY
Section 4 provides the punishments to which offenders are liable under BNS.

The punishments are:

   1. Death
   2. Imprisonment for life
   3. Imprisonment, which may be:
         ○ Rigorous, or
         ○ Simple
   4. Forfeiture of property
   5. Fine
   6. Community service

⭐ Important Change
The express inclusion of community service is one of the significant changes introduced by
BNS.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 27 / 748>
1.1 Death
Death penalty is the most severe punishment recognised by BNS.

It is imposed only for offences where the relevant provision specifically authorises it.

Constitutional position
The death penalty has been upheld as constitutionally permissible, but the Supreme Court has
repeatedly held that it should be imposed only in the “rarest of rare” cases.

⚖️ Important Case
Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab

The Supreme Court established the rarest of rare doctrine for imposition of the death penalty.

Exam point
Do not write:

      Death penalty can never be imposed.

Instead write:

      Death penalty is constitutionally permissible but its imposition is subject to the
      principles developed by the Supreme Court, particularly the rarest of rare doctrine.




1.2 Imprisonment for Life
Imprisonment for life means imprisonment for the remainder of the offender's natural life, subject
to lawful remission, commutation or other applicable provisions.

⚠️ Important
Do not automatically equate:

      Life imprisonment = 14 years

This is a common examination mistake.

The 14-year concept arises in particular statutory contexts concerning release/remission and
does not mean that every life sentence automatically terminates after 14 years.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 28 / 748>
⚖️ Important Case
Gopal Vinayak Godse v. State of Maharashtra
The Supreme Court explained the legal nature of imprisonment for life.

⭐ Exam point:
      Life imprisonment ordinarily means imprisonment for the remainder of the convict's

   ●    in addition to imprisonment.

CHAPTER III – GENERAL EXCEPTIONS
BNS Sections: 14–44

🔴 EXAM PRIORITY: VERY HIGH
General Exceptions are among the most important concepts in substantive criminal law.
They explain circumstances in which an act that would otherwise constitute an offence is not
treated as criminal.

The official BNS arrangement places Sections 14–44 under Chapter III, General Exceptions.




1. MEANING OF GENERAL EXCEPTIONS
Normally, when the ingredients of an offence are proved, criminal liability follows.

However, the law recognises circumstances in which a person should not be held criminally
liable, despite the apparent commission of an act.

These circumstances are called General Exceptions.

Examples
A person may avoid criminal liability where:

   ●   the act was done under a legal obligation;
   ●   it was a genuine accident;
   ●   the person was legally incapable of understanding the nature of the act;
   ●   the person was a child below the prescribed age;
   ●   the act was done in the exercise of lawful private defence;
   ●   the act was done with legally valid consent in circumstances recognised by law.

⭐ Basic principle
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 44 / 748>
      General Exceptions operate as defences to criminal liability where their
      statutory conditions are satisfied.




2. SECTION 14 – ACT DONE BY A
PERSON BOUND, OR BY MISTAKE OF
FACT BELIEVING HIMSELF BOUND, BY
LAW
BNS Section: 14
Corresponding IPC: §76

🔴 MUST STUDY
Section 14 protects a person who:

   1. is actually bound by law to do an act; or
   2. because of a mistake of fact, in good faith believes that he is bound by law to do the
      act.

However, the mistake must be a mistake of fact, not a mistake of law.




2.1 Mistake of Fact
A person may be protected where his mistake concerns a factual circumstance.

Example
A police officer receives a lawful order to arrest Y.

After making reasonable enquiry, A honestly and in good faith believes Z is Y and arrests Z.

A may receive the protection of Section 14 because his mistake concerns identity—a fact.

The BNS itself provides an illustration substantially along these lines.




2.2 Mistake of Law
A person cannot ordinarily escape criminal liability merely by saying:
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 45 / 748>
       “I did not know that the law prohibited my act.”

⭐ Maxim
       Ignorantia juris non excusat

Meaning:

       Ignorance of law is no excuse.

                                                     prohibited

6. SECTION 18 – ACCIDENT IN DOING A

BNS Section: 18
Corresponding IPC: §80
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 48 / 748>
🔴 IMPORTANT
An act is not an offence when it is done:

   1.    accidentally;
   2.    without criminal intention or knowledge;
   3.    while doing a lawful act;
   4.    in a lawful manner;
   5.    by lawful means; and
   6.    with proper care and caution.

Formula
        Lawful act + lawful manner + lawful means + proper care + accident + no
        criminal intent/knowledge = protection




Example
A is chopping wood with proper care and caution.

A tool unexpectedly slips and injures B.

If all statutory conditions are satisfied, A may be protected under the accident exception.




⚠️ Important

        “It was an accident”

is not enough.

The prosecution/defence must be examined against the statutory requirements.

10. SECTION 22 – ACT OF A PERSON OF

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 51 / 748>

10.1 Legal Insanity vs Medical Insanity
This is one of the most important distinctions in criminal law.

Medical insanity
A medical/psychiatric condition diagnosed by medical science.

Legal insanity
The specific mental incapacity required by the criminal law at the time of commission of the
act.

⭐ Important
       Every medically insane person is not necessarily legally insane.

The question is whether the statutory test is satisfied at the relevant time.




10.2 Time of Insanity
The critical point is:

       The mental condition at the time of commission of the act.

A person having a history of mental illness does not automatically qualify for the defence.

Principle
The accused bears the burden of bringing the case within the statutory exception, but the
prosecution continues to bear the ultimate burden of proving the offence beyond reasonable
doubt.

Exam use

McNaghten's Principles
The traditional legal test of insanity is associated with the McNaghten Rules.

The central idea is whether, because of mental disease, the accused was incapable of knowing:

   ●    the nature and quality of the act; or
   ●    that the act was wrong.

These principles influenced the development of the Indian statutory rule now reflected in BNS
§22.

PRIVATE DEFENCE
BNS Section: 34
Corresponding IPC: §96

🔴 EXTREMELY IMPORTANT
Section 34 establishes the basic principle:

       Nothing is an offence which is done in the exercise of the right of private
       defence.

The detailed rules are contained in the following sections.




23. SECTION 35 – THINGS DONE IN
PRIVATE DEFENCE OF BODY AND
PROPERTY
BNS Section: 35
Corresponding IPC: §97

A person has a right of private defence of:

A. His own body
and

B. The body of any other person
The right also extends to:

C. Property
against specified offences involving:

   ●    human body;
   ●    movable property;
   ●    immovable property.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 60 / 748>
24. SECTION 36 – RIGHT OF PRIVATE
DEFENCE AGAINST ACT OF PERSON OF
UNSOUND MIND, ETC.
BNS Section: 36
Corresponding IPC: §98

The right of private defence can arise even where the person posing the threat is legally
incapable of criminal responsibility because of circumstances such as:

    ●    infancy;
    ●    unsoundness of mind;
    ●    intoxication;
    ●    mistake.

⭐ Principle
        The right of private defence depends upon the nature of the threat, not merely upon
        the criminal liability of the aggressor.




25. SECTION 37 – ACTS AGAINST WHICH
THERE IS NO RIGHT OF PRIVATE
DEFENCE
BNS Section: 37
Corresponding IPC: §99

🔴 VERY IMPORTANT
The right of private defence has limitations.

There is no unlimited right to inflict harm.

The provision restricts the defence in specified circumstances, including situations where:

    ●    there is no reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt;
    ●    there is sufficient opportunity to seek protection from public authorities;
    ●    the force used exceeds what is legally permitted.

⭐ Golden principle
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 61 / 748>
       Private defence is defensive, not retaliatory.

It is meant to protect a person or property, not to take revenge.




26. SECTION 38 – WHEN RIGHT OF
PRIVATE DEFENCE OF BODY EXTENDS
TO CAUSING DEATH
BNS Section: 38
Corresponding IPC: §100

The right of private defence of the body can extend to causing death where the circumstances
specified by law exist.

These include apprehension of serious offences such as:

   ●    death;
   ●    grievous hurt;
   ●    rape;
   ●    kidnapping or abduction;
   ●    wrongful confinement under circumstances creating serious danger;
   ●    certain acts involving acid attack.

⭐ Important
The right to cause death is exceptional.

It does not arise merely because someone has insulted, threatened or caused minor harm.




27. SECTION 39 – WHEN SUCH RIGHT
EXTENDS TO CAUSING ANY HARM
OTHER THAN DEATH
BNS Section: 39
Corresponding IPC: §101

Where the circumstances do not justify causing death, the right of private defence may still
permit causing harm short of death, subject to the statutory conditions.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 62 / 748>
Simple distinction

§38 → Death may be caused

§39 → Harm other than death




28. SECTION 40 – COMMENCEMENT AND
CONTINUANCE OF RIGHT OF PRIVATE
DEFENCE OF BODY
BNS Section: 40
Corresponding IPC: §102

The right begins when a reasonable apprehension of danger to the body arises.

It continues as long as that apprehension continues.

⭐ Important
The right does not require waiting until the actual attack has occurred.




29. SECTION 41 – WHEN RIGHT OF
PRIVATE DEFENCE OF PROPERTY
EXTENDS TO CAUSING DEATH
BNS Section: 41
Corresponding IPC: §103

In specified serious property-related situations, the right of private defence of property may
extend to causing death.

These include certain circumstances involving:

   ●   robbery;
   ●   house-breaking after sunset and before sunrise;
   ●   mischief by fire/explosive to certain property;
   ●   theft/mischief/house-trespass under circumstances creating reasonable apprehension of
       death or grievous hurt.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 63 / 748>
30. SECTION 42 – WHEN SUCH RIGHT
EXTENDS TO CAUSING ANY HARM
OTHER THAN DEATH
BNS Section: 42
Corresponding IPC: §104

Where the circumstances do not fall within Section 41, the right of private defence of property
may extend to causing harm other than death, subject to the statutory conditions.




31. SECTION 43 – COMMENCEMENT AND
CONTINUANCE OF RIGHT OF PRIVATE
DEFENCE OF PROPERTY
BNS Section: 43
Corresponding IPC: §105

The right of private defence of property begins and continues according to the circumstances
and nature of the threat.

Different rules apply depending upon whether the conduct involves:

   ●   theft;
   ●   robbery;
   ●   criminal trespass;
   ●   house-breaking;
   ●   mischief.

⭐ Exam point
The right is not automatically available indefinitely merely because property was previously
threatened.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 64 / 748>
32. SECTION 44 – RIGHT OF PRIVATE
DEFENCE AGAINST DEADLY ASSAULT
WHEN THERE IS RISK OF HARM TO AN
INNOCENT PERSON
BNS Section: 44
Corresponding IPC: §106

This is a specialised private-defence provision.

It applies where a person faces a deadly assault and cannot effectively exercise private
defence without creating some risk of harm to an innocent person.

The law recognises the difficult situation and provides protection subject to its conditions.

CHAPTER IV – ABETMENT, CRIMINAL
CONSPIRACY AND ATTEMPT
BNS Sections: 45–62

🔴 EXAM PRIORITY: VERY HIGH
This chapter contains three foundational concepts of criminal liability:

   1. Abetment
   2. Criminal conspiracy
   3. Attempt

These concepts are important because a person can incur criminal liability even where he does
not personally complete the principal offence.




PART A – ABETMENT

SECTION 45 – ABETMENT OF A THING
BNS Section: 45
Corresponding IPC: §107

🔴 MUST STUDY
A person abets the doing of a thing if he:

   1. instigates another person to do that thing; or
   2. engages with one or more persons in a conspiracy for doing that thing, provided an act
      or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy; or
   3. intentionally aids, by an act or illegal omission, the doing of that thing.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 72 / 748>
Therefore:

        Abetment = Instigation + Conspiracy + Intentional Aid

⭐ Memorise this formula
I + C + A = Abetment




1. INSTIGATION
Instigation means provoking, inciting, urging or encouraging another person to do an act.

It may be:

    ●    direct; or
    ●    indirect.

Example
A tells B:

        “Go and kill C. I will take care of everything afterwards.”

If A's conduct amounts to intentional encouragement or provocation leading to the offence, A
may be liable for abetment by instigation.




2. CONSPIRACY AS A FORM OF
ABETMENT
Abetment may also occur where a person:

    ●    joins a conspiracy with another person or persons; and
    ●    an act or illegal omission occurs in pursuance of that conspiracy.

⚠️ Important distinction
Conspiracy under Section 61 is itself a substantive offence.

But conspiracy can also constitute one method of abetment under Section 45.

Do not confuse the two.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 73 / 748>
3. INTENTIONAL AID
A person may abet an offence by intentionally helping its commission.

Aid may be provided through:

   ●   a positive act; or
   ●   an illegal omission.

Example
A knows that B intends to commit robbery and intentionally provides B with the keys needed to
enter the premises.

A may have intentionally aided the offence.

SECTION 46 – ABETTOR
BNS Section: 46
Corresponding IPC: §108

An abettor is a person who abets:

   ●   the commission of an offence; or
   ●   an act which would be an offence if committed by a person capable of committing an
       offence with the same intention or knowledge as that of the abettor.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 74 / 748>
⭐ Important
The person who actually commits the principal offence is not necessarily the only person who
can be criminally liable.

The abettor may independently incur liability.




SECTION 47 – ABETMENT IN INDIA OF
OFFENCE OUTSIDE INDIA
BNS Section: 47
Corresponding IPC: §108A

This provision addresses situations where:

       The act of abetment occurs in India, but the offence is committed outside
       India.

Example
A, while in India, intentionally assists B in committing an offence in another country.

Where the statutory requirements are fulfilled, BNS may apply to A's abetment.

⭐ Exam point
       Abetment can have an extra-territorial dimension.
