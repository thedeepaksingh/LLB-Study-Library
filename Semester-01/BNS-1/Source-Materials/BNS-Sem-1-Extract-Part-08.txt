UNIT I – GENERAL PRINCIPLES
🔴 Q1. What is Mens Rea?
Answer:
Mens rea means the guilty mind accompanying the commission of a criminal act. Depending
upon the offence, it may take the form of intention, knowledge, dishonesty, fraud, recklessness
or negligence.

Mens rea is a fundamental principle of criminal liability because a person should ordinarily not
be punished merely for an innocent act. However, the requirement depends upon the wording
and nature of the particular offence.

⭐ Exam line:
       Mens rea refers to the legally required mental element of an offence.




🔴 Q2. What is Actus Reus?
Answer:
Actus reus means the physical element of a crime. It may consist of:

   ●    a positive act;
   ●    an omission where there is a legal duty to act; or
   ●    a prohibited state of affairs,
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 546 / 748>
depending upon the offence.

For criminal liability, the actus reus must satisfy the requirements of the particular statutory
offence.

⭐ Formula:
         Actus Reus = Physical element of crime




🔴sit rea.
     Q3. Explain the maxim Actus non facit reum nisi mens

Answer:
The maxim means:

         "An act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty."

It expresses the general principle that criminal liability ordinarily requires both:

    ●     a prohibited act; and
    ●     the required guilty mental state.

However, the principle is subject to statutory exceptions.




🔴 Q4. What is Common Intention?
BNS §3(5)
Common intention exists where several persons participate in a criminal act in furtherance of a
common intention of all.

Each participant may be liable as if he had done the act himself, subject to the statutory
requirements.

Essentials
    1.    Two or more persons.
    2.    Common intention.
    3.    Participation.
    4.    Act in furtherance of common intention.

⚖️ Case
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 547 / 748>
Mahbub Shah v Emperor

BNS §§189–190
Common object is the shared object of an unlawful assembly of five or more persons.

Members can incur constructive liability for offences committed in prosecution of the common
object, or which they knew were likely to be committed, subject to §190.

⭐ Key point
       Common object → 5 or more persons

🔴 Q7. What is an unlawful omission?
An omission becomes criminal where:

   ●     the law imposes a legal duty to act; and
   ●     failure to act satisfies the ingredients of the offence.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 548 / 748>
Example
A person legally responsible for providing necessary care deliberately fails to perform a legally
enforceable duty in circumstances creating criminal liability.

⭐ Point
        Every failure to act is not automatically a crime.




🔴 Q8. What are the stages of crime?
Four stages:

   1.    Intention
   2.    Preparation
   3.    Attempt
   4.    Commission

Formula

        I→P→A→C

Mere intention is generally not punishable.

Preparation is generally not punishable unless specifically criminalised.

Attempt is ordinarily punishable.

Commission completes the offence.




🔴 Q9. What is Preparation?
Preparation means:

        Arranging the means or measures necessary to commit a crime.

Example
A purchases tools intending to break into a house.

Ordinarily, this is preparation and not yet attempt.

🔴 Q10. What is Attempt?
Attempt begins when a person:

   ●    has the required criminal intention; and
   ●    performs an act sufficiently connected with the commission of the offence,

but the offence is not completed.

BNS §62
Section 62 provides the general punishment framework for attempts where no specific provision
exists.

BNS §45
A person abets an act by:

   1. Instigating its commission;
   2. engaging in a legally relevant conspiracy for doing it; or
   3. intentionally aiding its commission.

Instigation means:

       Provoking, inciting, urging or encouraging another person to do an act.

The exact conduct must be assessed according to the statutory requirements.

Mere presence or ordinary words of advice do not automatically amount to instigation.

Under the specified General Exceptions, an honest mistake of fact may protect a person where

UNIT I – GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF
CRIMINAL LIABILITY
🔴 Q1. Explain the essential elements of a crime.
Answer
A crime is an act or omission prohibited by law and made punishable by the State. Criminal
liability generally requires both a prohibited conduct and a legally blameworthy mental element,
subject to offences of strict liability.

1. Human conduct
There must ordinarily be a voluntary act or legally punishable omission.

A person is not normally criminally liable merely for thoughts or intentions.

Example:
A intends to kill B but does nothing. Mere intention is ordinarily not murder.

2. Actus Reus
Actus reus means the physical element of the offence.

It may consist of:

   ●   an act;
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 582 / 748>
    ●    an omission where law imposes a duty;
    ●    a prohibited consequence; or
    ●    specified circumstances.

3. Mens Rea
Mens rea means the guilty mental state accompanying the prohibited conduct.

Depending upon the offence, it may involve:

    ●    intention;
    ●    knowledge;
    ●    reason to believe;
    ●    recklessness;
    ●    dishonesty;
    ●    fraudulence.

4. Concurrence of act and mental state
Generally, the guilty mental state must coincide with the prohibited conduct.

5. Causation
Where an offence requires a particular result, the prosecution must establish the required
causal connection between the accused's conduct and the result.

Example: In a homicide case, the prosecution must establish that the accused's conduct
caused the death.

6. Legality
No person can ordinarily be convicted unless the conduct was criminalised by law.

This is reflected in the principle:

        Nullum crimen sine lege — no crime without law.

7. Punishment
An act becomes a crime because the law attaches a criminal sanction to it.

⭐ Exam conclusion
Thus, the basic structure of criminal liability may be remembered as:

Conduct + prohibited circumstances/result + required mens rea + causation, where
applicable + absence of a valid defence = criminal liability.

🔴importance
    Q2. Explain Mens Rea and its
            in criminal law.
Answer
Mens rea literally means a guilty mind. It refers to the mental element required by the definition
of a criminal offence.

The general principle is:

       Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea.

It means an act does not ordinarily make a person criminally liable unless accompanied by a
guilty mind.

Forms of mens rea
1. Intention

Intention is the conscious objective to bring about a particular consequence.

Example: A deliberately shoots B intending to kill him.

2. Knowledge

A person may be liable where he knows that his conduct is likely to produce a prohibited
consequence.

3. Reason to believe

Some offences use the statutory standard of having reason to believe a particular fact.

4. Dishonesty

Dishonesty is particularly important in property offences such as theft and criminal breach of
trust.

5. Fraudulence

Fraudulent intention is relevant to offences involving deception and fraudulent conduct.

Importance
Mens rea:

   ●    distinguishes accidental conduct from intentional crime;
   ●    protects innocent persons;
   ●    helps determine the degree of criminal responsibility;
   ●    assists courts in interpreting statutory offences.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 584 / 748>
Exceptions
Mens rea is not an absolute requirement for every offence. Parliament may create offences
where liability is based substantially on the prohibited act or conduct.

⭐ Exam line
Mens rea is the mental element of criminal liability, but the exact mental state must be
determined from the ingredients of the particular offence.

    Q3. Explain the different stages of

Answer
The commission of a crime generally passes through four stages:

Intention → Preparation → Attempt → Commission




1. Intention
Intention is the mental decision to commit an offence.

Mere intention is ordinarily not punishable.

Example: A decides to kill B but takes no further step.




2. Preparation
Preparation means arranging the means or measures necessary for committing the offence.

Example: A purchases poison intending to kill B.

Preparation is generally not punishable, although specific statutes may criminalise particular
preparatory conduct.




3. Attempt
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 585 / 748>
Attempt begins when the accused moves beyond preparation and performs an act sufficiently
proximate to the commission of the offence.

Under BNS §62, attempt is punishable where the statutory requirements are satisfied.

Example: A fires a gun at B intending to kill him, but B survives because the shot misses.




4. Commission
The final stage is completion of all ingredients of the offence.

Example: A intentionally shoots B and B dies, satisfying the ingredients of murder.

Important distinction
    Stage           General position

 Intention      Generally not
                punishable
 Preparation    Generally not
                punishable
 Attempt        Generally punishable

 Commission     Fully punishable


Cases
⚖️ Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar
The Supreme Court explained the distinction between preparation and attempt.

⚖️ State of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub
The Court examined proximity between the act performed and commission of the intended
offence.

⭐ Exam formula
Intention is mental; preparation arranges the means; attempt is direct movement toward
commission; commission completes the offence.

     Q4. Explain the doctrine of common
           under BNS.
Answer
The principle of common intention is contained in BNS §3(5), corresponding broadly to IPC §34.

It deals with situations where several persons participate in a criminal act pursuant to a common
intention.

Essential ingredients
   1.   There must be two or more persons.
   2.   There must be a common intention.
   3.   The criminal act must be done in furtherance of that common intention.
   4.   There must be participation in the act.

Meaning of common intention
Common intention means a shared intention or meeting of minds to commit the criminal act.

The common intention may develop before the incident or, depending on circumstances, during
the course of events.

Example
A and B decide to attack C. A restrains C while B causes the fatal injury. If the act was done
pursuant to their common intention, both may incur liability for the criminal act.

Important point
Section 3(5) does not create a separate substantive offence. It is a principle of
constructive/joint liability.

Cases
⚖️ Mahbub Shah v Emperor
The Supreme Court stressed the requirement of a meeting of minds.

⚖️ Barendra Kumar Ghosh v King-Emperor
A participant may be liable even where he does not personally perform every physical act
constituting the offence, provided the statutory requirements of common intention are
established.

⭐ Exam line
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 587 / 748>
Common intention is different from merely having the same intention independently;
there must be a shared plan or meeting of minds accompanied by participation.

🔴factQ6.under

Answer
The BNS recognises certain circumstances where a person acts under a mistake of fact and
therefore lacks the required criminal intention.

Important provisions include BNS §§14 and 17.

Mistake of fact
A mistake of fact occurs when a person misunderstands an existing factual circumstance.

Example: A reasonably believes that B is a dangerous armed intruder and acts in
circumstances covered by the relevant statutory exception.

Mistake of law
Ignorance or misunderstanding of law generally does not excuse criminal liability.

The maxim is:

Ignorantia juris non excusat.

Essential principle
The mistake must generally be:

   ●   a mistake of fact, not law;
   ●   made in circumstances recognised by the statutory exception;
   ●   honestly held; and
   ●   sufficient to negate the required criminal intention where the provision so requires.

Cases
⚖️ State of Orissa v Ram Bahadur Thapa
The Court considered the accused's mistaken belief concerning facts in determining criminal
liability.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 589 / 748>
⭐ Exam distinction
Mistake of fact may excuse in circumstances recognised by law; mistake of law
ordinarily does not.




🔴under
   Q7. Explain the defence of accident
       BNS §18.
Answer
BNS §18 recognises the defence of accident in circumstances where harm occurs without
criminal intention or knowledge while a person is performing a lawful act in a lawful manner and
by lawful means, with proper care and caution.

Essential ingredients
   1.   There must be an accident.
   2.   The act must be lawful.
   3.   It must be done in a lawful manner.
   4.   Lawful means must be used.
   5.   There must be no criminal intention or knowledge.
   6.   Proper care and caution must have been exercised.

Example
A is lawfully performing a lawful act with proper care. An unexpected accident occurs causing
injury to another person. If the statutory requirements are fulfilled, criminal liability may be
excluded.

Important limitation
The defence is unavailable where the accused's conduct itself constitutes negligence or
recklessness of the kind required for criminal liability.

⭐ Exam line
Accident is a defence only when the harm is genuinely accidental and the statutory
requirements of lawful conduct and due care are satisfied.

🔴under
   Q8. Explain the defence of necessity
       BNS §19.
Answer
BNS §19 recognises circumstances involving an act done to prevent greater harm.

The principle is based upon the idea that criminal law should not ordinarily punish conduct
undertaken in circumstances where a person reasonably acts to avoid a greater harm.

Essential elements
   ●   There must be a situation involving danger or threatened harm.
   ●   The accused must act to prevent that harm.
   ●   The conduct must fall within the statutory protection.
   ●   The circumstances must justify the interference with another interest.

Example
A breaks open a locked door to rescue a person trapped inside a burning building. The property
damage may be justified if the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Important point
Necessity is not a blanket permission to commit offences. The precise statutory conditions must
be satisfied.

⭐ Exam distinction
Necessity involves choosing conduct to prevent a greater harm; accident involves
unintended harm arising during otherwise lawful conduct.

Medical insanity concerns medical diagnosis.

🔴intoxication
    Q11. Explain the defence of
              under BNS.
Answer
The BNS distinguishes between involuntary intoxication and intoxication voluntarily induced.

BNS §23 – Involuntary intoxication
Where intoxication is caused without the person's knowledge or against the person's will, the
law may protect the person where the statutory conditions are fulfilled.

BNS §24 – Intoxication and offences requiring particular knowledge or
intention
Voluntary intoxication does not generally provide a complete defence merely because the
accused was drunk.

Answer
The right of private defence is contained in BNS §§34–44.

It is based on the principle that a person should be entitled to protect:

   ●    his own body;
   ●    another person's body;
   ●    his own property;
   ●    another person's property,

   2.   The danger must arise from conduct covered by the law.
   3.   The right must be exercised while the danger continues.
   4.   The force used must remain within statutory limits.
   5.   The right cannot ordinarily be used for revenge.

Private defence may extend to circumstances involving serious offences and, in specified
cases, may extend to causing death.

The right may arise against offences such as:

   ●   robbery;
   ●   theft;
   ●   mischief;
   ●   criminal trespass;
   ●   house-trespass,

subject to statutory requirements.

Limitations
The right does not permit:

   ●   unlimited force;
   ●   retaliation after danger has ended;
   ●   revenge;
   ●   use of force disproportionate to the circumstances beyond what law permits.

Cases
⚖️ Darshan Singh v State of Punjab

🔴 Q13. Explain abetment under BNS §45.
Answer
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 595 / 748>
BNS §45 defines abetment, broadly corresponding to IPC §107.

   1. instigates another to do it;

Instigation involves provoking, inciting, urging or encouraging another person to do the act.

   ●   assistance;
   ●   facilitation;
   ●   supplying means;
   ●   deliberate omission where legally relevant.

🔴fromQ15.preparation.
            Explain attempt and distinguish it

Answer
Attempt is dealt with generally under BNS §62.

Attempt represents the stage between preparation and commission.

Essential elements
   1.   Intention to commit the offence.
   2.   Preparation toward commission.
   3.   An act beyond mere preparation.
   4.   The act must be sufficiently connected with the intended offence.
   5.   The offence is not completed.

Preparation consists of arranging the means necessary for committing the offence.

Attempt
Attempt begins when the accused takes a direct step toward execution.

Preparation provides the means; attempt begins execution.

Answer
This is one of the highest-priority questions for the examination.

BNS provisions
   ●   §100 – Culpable homicide
   ●   §101 – Murder
   ●   §103 – Punishment for murder
   ●   §105 – Culpable homicide not amounting to murder

Murder is an aggravated form of culpable homicide.

Culpable homicide
A person commits culpable homicide where death is caused with the intention or knowledge
specified in §100.

Murder
Under §101, culpable homicide becomes murder where the case falls within one of the statutory
clauses and is not protected by any of the five exceptions.

Four principal clauses of murder
   1. Intention to cause death.
   2. Intention to cause bodily injury known to be likely to cause death of that particular
      person.
   3. Intention to cause bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death.
   4. Knowledge that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause
      death or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, without excuse for incurring the
      risk.

Five exceptions
   ●   Grave and sudden provocation.
   ●   Exceeding right of private defence.
   ●   Public servant exceeding powers in good faith.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 599 / 748>
   ●   Sudden fight without premeditation.
   ●   Consent of person above eighteen to suffer death/risk of death.

Case
⚖️ State of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya
The Supreme Court explained:

“All murder is culpable homicide, but not all culpable homicide is murder.”

⚖️ Virsa Singh v State of Punjab
Important authority concerning the third clause relating to bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary
course of nature to cause death.

⭐ Exam conclusion
The correct approach is:

First determine culpable homicide → then determine whether §101 makes it murder

   ●   heat of passion;
   ●   sudden quarrel;

🔴malice.
   Q18. Explain the doctrine of transferred

Answer
The principle of transferred malice is reflected in BNS §102, corresponding broadly to IPC §301.

It applies where a person intends to cause death or culpable harm to one person but causes the
prohibited result to another.

Example
A shoots at B intending to kill B, but the bullet misses B and kills C.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 601 / 748>
A's intention toward B may be transferred so that liability is determined with reference to the
death of C, subject to the statutory provision.

Principle
The law does not ordinarily allow an accused to escape liability merely because the actual
victim was different from the intended victim.

Important point
The exact offence still depends upon:

   ●    the original intention/knowledge;
   ●    the actual result;
   ●    the statutory ingredients.

⭐ Exam line
Transferred malice transfers the legal effect of the guilty intention from the intended
victim to the actual victim, subject to the statutory conditions.
