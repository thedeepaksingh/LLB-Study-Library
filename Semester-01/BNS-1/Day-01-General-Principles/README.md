# Day 1 — General Principles of Crime

**Subject:** BNS-1 — Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023  
**Level:** Beginner  
**Language:** Simple English  
**Status:** Foundation lesson; verify statutory language in the official Bare Act.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- define crime in simple words;
- explain actus reus and mens rea;
- describe intention, preparation, attempt and commission;
- distinguish criminal wrongs from civil wrongs;
- explain at a basic level why statutory ingredients and defences matter.

## 1. Meaning of crime

A crime is conduct prohibited by law and punishable by the State. It may involve an act or a legally relevant omission. Criminal liability depends on the ingredients of the particular offence and any applicable exceptions or defences.

Examples:
- Dishonestly taking another person's movable property without consent may amount to theft if all statutory ingredients are fulfilled.
- Causing death may be culpable homicide or murder depending on the circumstances and statutory requirements.
- An attempt may be punishable where the law and the facts satisfy the requirements for attempt liability.

Criminal law protects public interests and provides a process for investigation, prosecution, trial and punishment. A criminal case can sometimes arise from the same facts as a civil claim; one type of liability does not always exclude the other.

## 2. Basic elements and terminology

### Actus reus — the external element

This is the prohibited conduct, together with any circumstances or result required by the relevant offence. It can include an act or, where a legal duty exists, an omission.

### Mens rea — the required mental element

This refers to a mental state required by the offence, such as intention, knowledge, dishonesty or rashness. The required mental state varies by offence.

**Important qualification:** Do not assume that every offence has the same mens rea requirement. Some offences impose strict liability for a particular element; always read the statutory definition.

### Person and applicable defences

Criminal liability is subject to legal rules about capacity and statutory exceptions. Age, unsoundness of mind and other circumstances can be relevant under specified provisions. A court must apply the actual legal requirements to the facts.

### Illustration

A picks up a bag believing honestly that it is A's identical bag. The physical act of taking the bag occurred, but the dishonest intention required for theft may be absent. The conclusion depends on evidence and the statutory ingredients.

## 3. The stages of crime

### Stage 1 — Intention
A person forms a mental decision to commit an offence. Intention alone is generally not punishable, though statements or conduct may be relevant evidence and some distinct offences can apply.

### Stage 2 — Preparation
A person arranges the means for committing an offence. Preparation is generally not punishable by itself, but there are statutory exceptions where preparatory conduct is independently criminalised.

### Stage 3 — Attempt
The person goes beyond mere preparation and takes a sufficiently direct step towards committing the offence, but the offence is not completed. Whether conduct amounts to attempt depends on the offence and facts.

### Stage 4 — Commission
The offence is completed when all of its legally required ingredients are fulfilled.

**Illustration:** Planning to steal a watch is intention. Obtaining tools may be preparation. Beginning to force open the cupboard to take the watch may constitute an attempt depending on the circumstances. Dishonestly taking the watch without consent, with the required ingredients, may complete theft.

## 4. Preparation versus attempt

| Preparation | Attempt |
|---|---|
| Arranging means for the offence | Moving sufficiently beyond preparation towards its commission |
| Usually an earlier stage | Usually a later stage |
| Generally not independently punishable, subject to exceptions | May be punishable under the applicable provision |
| Example: acquiring tools for a planned break-in | Example: starting to force the lock to steal the property |

Avoid treating this table as a mechanical rule: courts assess the conduct and the particular offence.

## 5. Crime and civil wrong

| Criminal law | Civil law |
|---|---|
| Focuses on public wrongs and punishment | Often focuses on private rights and remedies |
| Prosecution is generally conducted by the State | A claim is generally brought by an affected party |
| Outcomes may include statutory punishment | Outcomes may include damages, injunctions or other civil remedies |

A single act may give rise to criminal and civil proceedings if the separate requirements are met.

## 6. Exam-ready long answer

### Question: Define crime and explain its essential elements.

**Introduction:** Crime is conduct prohibited by law and punishable by the State. Criminal liability cannot be decided merely by asking whether an act was morally wrong; the legal ingredients must be established.

**External element:** The prosecution must establish the prohibited act or legally relevant omission and any circumstances or result required by the offence. This is commonly called *actus reus*.

**Mental element:** Where the offence requires a particular mental state, the prosecution must prove it to the required standard. This is commonly called *mens rea*. The exact requirement depends on the statutory definition.

**Other requirements:** The prosecution must establish all remaining elements. Relevant statutory exceptions and defences must be considered according to the law and the facts.

**Illustration:** In a theft allegation, the court examines the taking of movable property, the absence of consent and the required dishonest intention, together with the other statutory ingredients.

**Conclusion:** The essential elements of a crime are offence-specific. Actus reus and mens rea are useful general concepts, but the exact statutory ingredients determine criminal liability.

## 7. Important short notes

- **Actus reus:** External conduct, circumstances or result required by an offence.
- **Mens rea:** Mental element required by an offence.
- **Intention:** A decision or purpose to bring about conduct or a result; its legal meaning depends on context.
- **Attempt:** Conduct that crosses the legally relevant line beyond preparation towards commission of an offence.
- **Strict liability:** Liability for an offence or element without proof of the usual mental state for that part, where law so provides.
