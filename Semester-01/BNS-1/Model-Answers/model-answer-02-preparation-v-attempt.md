# Model Answer 02 — Distinguish Preparation from Attempt

**Question classification:** Predicted/model question; not a verified PYQ.

## Introduction

Intention, preparation, attempt and completion are commonly described as stages in the commission of crime. The legal difference between preparation and attempt matters because attempt may be punishable even when the intended offence is not completed.

## Preparation

Preparation involves arranging means or making arrangements to commit an offence. Preparation is generally not punishable by itself, but the law separately criminalises some preparatory conduct. The statement that preparation is never punishable would therefore be incorrect.

## Attempt

Attempt arises when conduct goes sufficiently beyond preparation towards committing an offence under the legal test applicable to that offence. The act need not succeed, but mere intention or planning is not automatically an attempt.

The BNS contains a general attempt provision in Section 62 for the cases it covers, while some offences have their own attempt provisions. The relevant specific provision must be checked before stating the punishment.

## Comparison

| Preparation | Attempt |
|---|---|
| Arranging means or making arrangements | Conduct crossing the legal boundary beyond preparation |
| Usually an earlier stage | Usually a later stage |
| Generally not independently punishable, subject to statutory exceptions | May be punishable under the applicable general or specific provision |
| The conduct may remain remote from execution | Conduct is more directly connected to execution of the offence |

## Illustration

A plans to take a watch and buys tools to open a cupboard. That may be preparation. If A starts forcing the cupboard open to take the watch, the conduct may cross the line into attempt depending on the facts and the offence.

## Conclusion

The distinction is fact-sensitive and offence-specific. An exam answer should identify the conduct performed and apply the correct statutory rule, rather than relying only on the accused's intention.
