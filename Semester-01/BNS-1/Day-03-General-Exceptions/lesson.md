# DAY-03 — Detailed Lesson: General Exceptions and Private Defence

**Study identifier:** DAY-03  
**Level:** Beginner  
**Question status:** The practice questions below are predicted, not verified past-paper questions.

## Learning outcomes

- Explain what a general exception does.
- Identify the conditions and limitations of common exceptions.
- Distinguish mistake of fact from mistake of law.
- Explain the right of private defence without treating it as unlimited permission to use force.

## 1. What is a general exception?

A general exception is a legal rule that may prevent conduct from being criminally punishable even when the conduct appears to meet some parts of an offence. It applies only when the statutory conditions are satisfied. An accused cannot rely on the name of a defence alone; the facts must meet the relevant legal test.

For each exception, identify:
1. the statutory provision;
2. the condition that must be satisfied;
3. facts supporting or contradicting that condition;
4. any limits or exclusions;
5. the conclusion on the stated facts.

## 2. Mistake of fact and mistake of law

The law distinguishes certain mistakes of fact made under legally relevant circumstances from a mistake about what the law says. A mistake of fact is not automatically a defence; the statutory requirements must be met, including any requirement of good faith or reasonable belief. Ignorance or misunderstanding of the law generally does not excuse an offence.

**Illustration:** A takes an umbrella believing, based on the circumstances, that it is their own identical umbrella. The claim may be relevant to dishonest intention. It is not the same as claiming that the law permits taking another person's umbrella.

## 3. Accident and necessity

An accident defence depends on the statutory requirements, including the nature of the act and whether it was done lawfully and with the legally required care or intention. A harmful result is not automatically a crime, but calling it an accident does not settle the issue.

Necessity provisions concern circumstances where an act is done to avoid greater harm, subject to the exact legal conditions. The defence is not an unrestricted licence to break the law whenever a person considers it useful.

## 4. Age and capacity

The BNS contains rules concerning acts of children and the legal relevance of age. Apply the relevant age category and the statute's exact test. Do not assume that a child is either always criminally responsible or always exempt.

## 5. Unsoundness of mind

The legal test concerns the person's mental condition at the legally relevant time and the requirements specified by the statute. A diagnosis alone does not automatically determine criminal responsibility. An exam answer should state the statutory test and apply the facts.

## 6. Intoxication

The Act distinguishes circumstances according to the applicable statutory rule. Voluntary intoxication does not automatically excuse criminal conduct. Where the exception for involuntary intoxication is considered, its statutory conditions must be examined carefully.

## 7. Consent and good faith

Consent and good faith can be relevant under specified provisions, but neither is a universal defence. The scope depends on the nature of the act, the person's capacity, the circumstances and the statutory restrictions. Some offences cannot be excused merely by asserting consent.

## 8. Right of private defence

Private defence protects a person against specified threats to body or property, subject to statutory rules. Analyse:
- Was there a legally relevant threat or danger?
- When did the right arise and when did it end?
- What action was legally permitted?
- Did the response remain within the statutory limits?
- Does the law permit the particular level of force in those circumstances?

**Defence versus retaliation:** Force used to repel a legally relevant danger is conceptually different from revenge after the danger has ended.

## Exam answer framework

**Question:** Explain the right of private defence.

**Introduction:** Private defence is a legal right to protect body or property in circumstances recognised by law.

**Statutory rule:** Identify the applicable BNS provisions and accurately state the conditions under which the right arises.

**Scope:** Explain the right in relation to the body and property separately, if the question requires it.

**Limits:** Discuss when the right begins and ends, restrictions on force, and circumstances in which the law permits a more serious response.

**Illustration:** A person facing an immediate unlawful attack may use force permitted by the statutory rule. The result depends on the danger, the response and the limits imposed by law.

**Conclusion:** Private defence is assessed from the statutory requirements and facts; it is not unlimited permission for retaliation.
