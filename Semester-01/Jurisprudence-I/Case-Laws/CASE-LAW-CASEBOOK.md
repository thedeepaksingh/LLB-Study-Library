# K-1001 — Case-Law Illustrations for Jurisprudence
**Use:** relate legal theory to decisions. Case law is illustrative here; the prescribed examination is organised around schools and thinkers.

Each case is summarised at a high level. Verify the report and current status before quoting a detailed holding or pinpoint citation.

## 1. Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973)
**Question illustrated:** Can constitutional amendment power be treated as legally unlimited?  
**Principle:** The Supreme Court's basic-structure doctrine holds that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution does not extend to destroying its basic structure.  
**Jurisprudential link:** Legal authority can be created and limited by a higher constitutional framework. It is useful for discussing Kelsenian hierarchy, constitutionalism and the difference between legal power and unlimited political power.  
**Exam caution:** Do not write that the judgment made every constitutional feature unamendable; the doctrine concerns the basic structure.

## 2. Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978)
**Question illustrated:** Does a legally authorised procedure automatically satisfy constitutional requirements?  
**Principle:** The Court interpreted Articles 14, 19 and 21 together and required a procedure affecting personal liberty to meet constitutional standards of fairness and non-arbitrariness.  
**Jurisprudential link:** Useful when discussing interpretation, the relationship between rules and justice, and how constitutional principles shape the content of legal rules.  
**Caution:** Explain the relevant constitutional doctrine; do not reduce the case to “natural law beats statute”.

## 3. Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India (2017)
**Question illustrated:** How can constitutional rights protect autonomy and dignity in a changing society?  
**Principle:** A nine-judge bench recognised privacy as a constitutionally protected fundamental right.  
**Jurisprudential link:** Demonstrates constitutional interpretation, rights-based reasoning and the way concepts of liberty and dignity develop through judicial reasoning.  
**Caution:** Cite the 2017 privacy judgment distinctly from later Puttaswamy decisions concerning specific schemes.

## 4. S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994)
**Question illustrated:** What legal limits govern the exercise of central political power over States?  
**Principle:** The judgment established important limits and judicial scrutiny concerning Article 356 and treated secularism as part of the Constitution's basic structure.  
**Jurisprudential link:** Useful for federalism, legal limits on sovereign power and sociological accounts of institutions.  
**Caution:** Explain the specific constitutional issue rather than saying the case resolves every federal dispute.

## 5. Minerva Mills Ltd. v Union of India (1980)
**Question illustrated:** Is constitutional power unlimited once a political institution holds it?  
**Principle:** The Court invalidated aspects of the constitutional amendment at issue and stressed limits on amending power and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.  
**Jurisprudential link:** Shows that legal rules can structure and constrain the institutions that make rules; it also supports discussion of constitutional values.  

## 6. ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976) and the later rejection of its approach
**Question illustrated:** Can formal state power override liberty during an emergency without meaningful judicial protection?  
**Principle:** The 1976 majority took a restrictive view of judicial enforcement of personal liberty during the Emergency; the approach was later expressly disapproved in *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017).  
**Jurisprudential link:** Useful for discussing legal positivism, legitimacy, morality and the danger of relying on legal authority without analysing rights and constitutional structure.  
**Caution:** Mention the later rejection; do not present the 1976 approach as today's controlling doctrine.

## 7. Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India (2018)
**Question illustrated:** How are constitutional rights and dignity interpreted in light of changing social understandings?  
**Principle:** The Supreme Court read down the application of former IPC section 377 to consensual sexual conduct between adults, while leaving other legally distinct categories untouched.  
**Jurisprudential link:** Useful for discussing constitutional morality, dignity, equal citizenship and the relationship between enacted law and ethical/constitutional reasoning. The statutory context has since changed; this is a jurisprudential illustration, not current advice about the BNS.

## 8. State of West Bengal v Union of India (1963)
**Question illustrated:** How should the nature of the Indian Union and the limits of State sovereignty be understood?  
**Principle:** The Court rejected an understanding of Indian States as retaining the same sovereign status as units in a compact of fully sovereign States and addressed the constitutional distribution of authority.  
**Jurisprudential link:** Useful as an illustration of constitutional positivism and institutional legal authority.  
**Caution:** For current federal questions, read the relevant constitutional provisions and later case law.

## How to use a case in a jurisprudence answer
A case should support a theory, not replace it. State the issue briefly, identify the legal idea, link it explicitly to the theorist/school, then give one limitation. For example: “*Kesavananda Bharati* illustrates the hierarchical and limited character of constitutional amendment power; it can assist a comparison with Kelsen's account of legal norms, though Kelsen's theory is not itself the basic-structure doctrine.”
