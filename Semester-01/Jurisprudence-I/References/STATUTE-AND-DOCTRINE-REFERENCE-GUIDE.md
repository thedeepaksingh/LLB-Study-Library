# K-1001 — Doctrine and Theory Reference Guide

Jurisprudence is not a single-statute subject. Its controlling classroom material is the syllabus, recommended jurisprudence text and the theory being discussed. Use this guide as a revision map, not as a claim that all listed thinkers are part of every unit in every edition.

## The seven required blocks
1. Introduction — definition, nature, scope, importance.
2. Natural law — classical thought; Stammler and Kohler.
3. Analytical — Austin, Kelsen, Hart.
4. Historical — Savigny and Maine.
5. Sociological — Pound and Duguit.
6. American modern realism.
7. Economic/Marxist approach.

## Terms to remember
- **Positive law:** law established/recognised through social or institutional sources in a legal system.
- **Sovereign (Austin):** determinate superior described through habitual obedience.
- **Sanction:** threatened legal consequence attached to a command in Austin's model.
- **Grundnorm (Kelsen):** presupposed basic norm at the foundation of a hierarchy of legal validity.
- **Primary rules (Hart):** conduct-guiding rules.
- **Secondary rules (Hart):** rules for identifying, changing and adjudicating primary rules.
- **Rule of recognition (Hart):** social rule used by officials to identify valid legal standards.
- **Volksgeist (Savigny):** a people's historical/legal consciousness, expressed through custom and institutions.
- **Status to contract (Maine):** broad proposition about a movement from relations fixed by status toward relations organised by agreement.
- **Social engineering (Pound):** ordering/balancing competing interests through legal institutions.
- **Social solidarity (Duguit):** social interdependence and the social function of public authority.
- **Legal realism:** examination of how legal institutions and officials actually decide disputes.
- **Marxist approach:** study of law in relation to class, property, production and economic power.

## A reliable theory-comparison structure
For any theory, write: **problem it addresses → main proposition → key terms → thinker → value → criticism → comparison**. The criticism should address the theory's reasoning, not merely say “it is wrong”.

## Case-law illustrations (not the centre of K-1001)
- *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* — constitutional limits and basic structure.
- *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* — constitutional interpretation and fair procedure.
- *K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India* — privacy, dignity and constitutional values.
- *Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India* — constitutional morality and rights.
- *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India* (2017) — privacy; later judgments are distinct decisions in the same broad litigation.

These cases illustrate debates about validity, constitutionalism, justice and legal interpretation. They do not replace the theorists named in the K-1001 syllabus.
