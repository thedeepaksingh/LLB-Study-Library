# K-1001 — Original Exam Practice and Worked Answers
**All prompts below are original study exercises. They are not verified CCSU previous-year questions.**

## Part A — Long-answer plans

### PRACTICE-JUR-01. Define jurisprudence and explain its nature, scope and importance.
**Plan:** Define jurisprudence as systematic theoretical study of law; note there is no single universally accepted definition; explain analytical, normative, historical and sociological approaches; set out the field's scope; explain interpretation, comparison, reform and critical analysis; conclude that different schools provide complementary but sometimes competing perspectives.

### PRACTICE-JUR-02. Explain natural law and its revival through Stammler and Kohler.
**Plan:** Give the broad link between law, reason and justice; describe classical natural-law ideas; explain Stammler's search for a just form responsive to changing circumstances; explain Kohler's cultural context; evaluate moral disagreement and abstraction. Do not treat the two revival theories as identical.

### PRACTICE-JUR-03. Critically examine Austin's command theory.
**Plan:** Define command, sovereign, duty and sanction; explain habitual obedience; note the theory's clarity about authority; evaluate constitutional limits, power-conferring rules, custom and continuity of law. Conclude that the theory captures one important aspect of law but is incomplete as a general account of modern legal systems.

### PRACTICE-JUR-04. Explain Kelsen's Pure Theory and Grundnorm.
**Plan:** Explain the normative character of law; hierarchy of norms; validity of lower norms traced to higher authorising norms; Grundnorm as a presupposition; distinguish validity from moral merit; evaluate abstraction and the difficulty of grounding the system.

### PRACTICE-JUR-05. Explain Hart's primary and secondary rules and compare them with Austin.
**Plan:** Define both categories; identify recognition, change and adjudication; explain the internal point of view; compare with command/sanction; identify why this explains constitutional and private-law powers better; note debates over social acceptance and morality.

### PRACTICE-JUR-06. Discuss Savigny's historical school.
**Plan:** Explain Volksgeist, custom, legal development and juristic work; value of historical context; risk that an alleged common spirit can obscure conflict and preserve injustice; cautious conclusion about organic legal development.

### PRACTICE-JUR-07. Discuss Maine's “status to contract” proposition.
**Plan:** Explain status and contract; put the statement as a historical generalisation about changing social relations; use family/social organisation as an illustration; consider regulation, inequality and reverse movements; avoid describing the thesis as universal and inevitable.

### PRACTICE-JUR-08. Explain Pound's social engineering and Duguit's social solidarity.
**Plan:** Explain interest balancing (Pound) and interdependence/social function (Duguit); similarities in attention to society; distinctions; practical value; criticisms about how competing interests are ranked or who decides the public function.

### PRACTICE-JUR-09. What is American legal realism? Evaluate it.
**Plan:** Explain the focus on how institutions actually decide; discuss Holmes/Llewellyn/Frank carefully; describe prediction and the role of facts/procedure; recognise why rules still constrain legal decisions; evaluate the danger of reducing law to arbitrary judicial preference.

### PRACTICE-JUR-10. Explain the Marxist/economic approach to law.
**Plan:** Explain class, property, production and legal institutions; show how rules may preserve economic arrangements; consider examples from property/contract/labour; explain the approach's critical contribution; evaluate economic reductionism and law's relative autonomy.

## Part C — Application questions

### Problem 1: The unfair command
A ruler orders a person to surrender property without any legal procedure. An official says, “The ruler's command is law, so justice is irrelevant.”
**Worked answer:** Austin's model can help explain the emphasis on authority, command and sanction, but it does not establish that every command is valid within a modern constitutional system or morally justified. Apply the rules of the relevant legal system to identify competence, procedure and constitutional limits. Natural-law analysis asks whether the command meets moral standards; Hart's model asks which recognised rules identify and constrain officials; Kelsen's model asks whether the command was authorised by higher norms. The best answer compares the frameworks instead of declaring one label decisive.

### Problem 2: A long-standing custom
A community asserts that a historic custom must be accepted as law even though it disadvantages one section of the community.
**Worked answer:** Savigny's theory highlights the historical development of law through custom, so the custom's history matters. But historical origin does not by itself establish legal validity or justice. The court or legislature must consider the recognised sources and constitutional/statutory limits. A natural-law or rights-based analysis may question injustice; a sociological analysis examines the real distribution of interests and harms. Explain the relevance of Savigny and then his theory's limitation.

### Problem 3: Two readings of a legal rule
A statute is ambiguous. One student claims its literal text always produces only one answer; another says judges can decide anything.
**Worked answer:** Both claims are overstatements. Analytical work stresses concepts, wording and sources; Hart recognises open texture and difficult cases; realism explores how evidence, procedure and judicial practice influence outcomes. Yet statutes, precedent, jurisdiction and constitutional rules constrain judges. A strong answer explains uncertainty without claiming either mechanical certainty or unlimited discretion.

### Problem 4: Economic inequality
A standard contract formally gives both parties identical rights, but one party has no realistic bargaining power.
**Worked answer:** The Marxist approach asks how economic structure and material power affect the relationship; Pound's sociological method examines competing interests and the social effects of contract rules; natural-law analysis asks about justice. A complete legal answer would also require actual contract doctrine and facts, but for jurisprudence the point is to demonstrate how different schools diagnose the same social problem differently.

### Problem 5: Government power
An official says government action is valid merely because it came from government, and therefore no legal limit can apply.
**Worked answer:** The assertion confuses the existence of public power with its legal limits. Kelsen's hierarchy highlights that lower decisions need authority under higher norms; Hart's secondary rules explain competence and adjudication; constitutional cases illustrate review. Duguit adds a social-function critique of public authority. The appropriate approach is to identify the source of power and relevant limitations.
