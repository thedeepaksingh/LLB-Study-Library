# Jurisprudence–I (K-1001): Complete Syllabus Teaching Notes
**Beginner edition · CCSU LL.B. Semester 1 · Simple English**

> These notes explain every heading listed for K-1001 in the located CCSU syllabus. They are a teaching aid, not a substitute for the recommended textbook or your lecturer's directions. Jurisprudence is theoretical: many questions ask you to explain and assess a theory, not to list definitions.

## 1. Introduction: meaning, nature, scope and importance

### 1.1 What is jurisprudence?
Jurisprudence is the systematic study of law at a general and theoretical level. It asks questions such as: What is law? Why is law binding? How is law related to morality? How do judges decide hard cases? What makes a legal system legitimate? What social purposes should law serve?

There is no single universally accepted definition because scholars approach law from different angles. John Austin famously treated jurisprudence as the philosophy of positive law. Other approaches study law's moral foundation, history, social effects, economic role or actual operation by courts.

### 1.2 Nature of jurisprudence
- **Analytical:** clarifies legal concepts and propositions.
- **Normative/critical:** asks what law ought to be and whether it is just.
- **Historical:** studies how legal institutions and rules develop.
- **Sociological/empirical:** examines how law works in society.
- **Interdisciplinary:** connects law with philosophy, politics, economics, history and sociology.

Different schools give different weights to these methods. A positivist may focus on legal validity; a natural-law thinker on justice; a realist on how institutions actually decide disputes.

### 1.3 Scope
The field can include the nature and sources of law, legal authority, legal validity, law and morality, interpretation, legal institutions, and the purposes/effects of law. For this paper, keep the syllabus priority on the listed schools and named thinkers. Detailed legal concepts may appear in other jurisprudence papers, so do not let them displace K-1001 topics.

### 1.4 Importance
1. It helps explain why a rule counts as law and who can make it.
2. It improves interpretation by exposing hidden assumptions and competing readings.
3. It helps compare legal systems and historical periods.
4. It shows the relationship between law, morality, power, custom and social change.
5. It encourages critical thinking about injustice and reform.
6. It helps lawyers explain not only the result of a case but the theory of legal authority behind it.

**Exam structure:** definition → nature/scope → importance → one or two contrasting viewpoints → conclusion.

## 2. Natural Law School

### 2.1 Core idea
Natural-law theories argue that law is connected in an important way to reason, morality, justice or principles that do not depend solely on the command of a political authority. The exact connection differs among natural-law writers. Do not write that every natural-law thinker holds that every unjust enactment automatically ceases to exist as a fact. Some distinguish legal existence from moral legitimacy.

### 2.2 Classical natural law
Classical accounts connect law with reason, nature and the common good. In the Aristotelian tradition, justice and practical reason are relevant to human flourishing. In the Thomistic tradition, Thomas Aquinas distinguishes eternal law, natural law, human law and divine law. Natural law is the rational creature's participation in eternal law; human law should be directed toward the common good and should be consistent with higher principles.

**Importance:** it supplies a moral standard for evaluating enacted law and helps explain the idea that power alone does not make a rule just.

**Criticisms:**
- People disagree about the content of natural law.
- Moral reasoning may not yield a single clear answer to every legal dispute.
- It can be difficult to identify which authority should decide that a positive law is inconsistent with natural law.
- Legal certainty may suffer if the theory is used without a disciplined method.

### 2.3 Revival of natural law: Rudolf Stammler
Stammler responded to the idea that law can be explained solely by social or economic facts. He searched for formal principles of a “just law” that could guide the evaluation of changing positive law. The emphasis is on a just method or form applied to changing social materials, rather than on a complete unchanging catalogue of detailed rules.

**Memory line:** Stammler — revival of natural law; search for a just form of law in changing circumstances.

**Critical point:** the ideal can be abstract, and deciding exactly what justice requires remains difficult.

### 2.4 Revival of natural law: Kohler
Kohler's approach recognises culture and civilisation as important to legal ideas. Law must be understood in relation to the cultural development and needs of a society rather than as a timeless set of highly specific rules detached from human experience.

**Memory line:** Kohler — law and justice must be understood in cultural context.

### 2.5 Natural law compared with legal positivism
Natural-law thinking asks whether law is morally justified and whether there is a necessary link between law and moral principles. Legal positivism asks what the recognised legal sources are and what makes a rule legally valid within a system. This is a broad contrast, not a claim that positivists do not care about morality or that natural lawyers ignore enacted statutes.

| Point | Natural-law approach | Analytical positivist approach |
|---|---|---|
| Central question | What connection exists between law, reason and justice? | What makes a rule valid law in a legal system? |
| Evaluation | Moral principles are central | Validity and moral merit can be separated analytically |
| Strength | Supplies a critical standard for injustice | Clarifies sources, authority and legal certainty |
| Risk | Moral disagreement can create uncertainty | May insufficiently explain legitimacy if treated too narrowly |

## 3. Analytical School

The analytical school examines legal rules, concepts and institutions as they operate in a legal system. It tries to describe positive law precisely. The CCSU syllabus names Austin, Kelsen and Hart.

### 3.1 John Austin: command theory
Austin's command theory presents positive law, in its central model, as a command issued by a determinate sovereign to political inferiors and backed by a sanction. Sovereignty is described in terms of habitual obedience and not itself habitually obedient to another earthly superior.

**Key terms**
- **Command:** expression of a wish by a superior that another act or refrain from acting.
- **Duty:** the position of a person exposed to the command and sanction.
- **Sanction:** threatened consequence for non-compliance.
- **Sovereign:** the determinate superior habitually obeyed by the political community.

**Contribution:** Austin makes the questions of authority, duty and sanction explicit and encourages precision about positive law.

**Criticisms**
1. It struggles with power-conferring rules, such as rules enabling people to make wills or contracts.
2. Constitutional limits and a modern division of powers do not fit easily with a single unlimited sovereign.
3. Customs and international law complicate the simple command model.
4. A law may persist through a change of ruler or be complied with without fear of sanction.
5. A threat-based account does not by itself establish that a rule is legitimate or just.

**How to evaluate:** explain the model fairly before criticising it. “Austin said law is a command” is only the opening of an answer, not an evaluation.

### 3.2 Hans Kelsen: Pure Theory of Law
Kelsen aimed to explain law as a normative order without reducing legal validity to morality, political preference or sociology. A legal norm derives validity from a higher norm under the system's rules. The structure is commonly drawn as a hierarchy of norms. The theory ultimately presupposes a basic norm (**Grundnorm**) that allows the legal order to be understood as valid.

**Key points**
- Law deals with norms about what ought legally to be done, rather than only facts about what people actually do.
- The validity of a lower norm is traced to a higher authorising norm.
- The Grundnorm is a presupposed basic norm, not simply one more written section of an ordinary statute.
- Coercion and sanctions are relevant to Kelsen's account of a legal order, but the theory is not identical to Austin's command by a sovereign.

**Contributions:** explains legal hierarchy, helps separate legal validity from moral approval, and gives a framework for constitutional supremacy.

**Criticisms:** the Grundnorm can seem abstract or circular; the theory can understate social and political conditions that explain the effectiveness of a legal system; and it does not by itself tell us whether a valid norm is just.

**Exam tip:** use the spelling “Kelsen”; some old student materials misspell it as “Kelson”. In your answer, accurately identify the Grundnorm and the hierarchy of norms.

### 3.3 H.L.A. Hart: law as a union of rules
Hart criticised the idea that law is merely a command backed by a threat. A legal system is a union of **primary rules** and **secondary rules**.

- **Primary rules:** require or prohibit conduct, such as rules against theft.
- **Secondary rules:** rules about how primary rules are identified, changed and applied.
- **Rule of recognition:** social rule used by officials to identify valid legal standards.
- **Rule of change:** determines how legal rules may be introduced, changed or repealed.
- **Rule of adjudication:** confers authority and procedure for deciding disputes.
- **Internal point of view:** participants treat rules as standards that guide or criticise conduct, not merely as predictions of punishment.

**Why Hart matters:** he explains how a legal system can include constitutions, legislatures, courts, power-conferring rules and accepted official practices better than a simple model of repeated commands.

**Criticisms and debate:** a rule of recognition depends on official social practice; the theory's account of the relation between law and morality has been debated. Hart does not say every law is morally good. His work also generated debate with Lon Fuller about the morality associated with legality.

### 3.4 Austin, Kelsen and Hart — examination comparison

| Question | Austin | Kelsen | Hart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central model | Command + sovereign + sanction | Hierarchy of valid norms + Grundnorm | Primary and secondary rules |
| Legal authority | Sovereign command | Validity derived through a normative order | Social rule of recognition and institutional rules |
| Strength | Makes authority and sanction visible | Explains hierarchy and validity | Explains complex modern systems and legal powers |
| Main weakness | Difficult fit with constitutional/power-conferring rules | Abstract foundation; validity not same as justice | Depends on accepted practice; debates about hard cases and morality |

## 4. Historical School

The historical school explains law through historical development, custom and the life of a society. It challenges the notion that every legal system can be fully understood as a product of a legislator's deliberate choice at one moment.

### 4.1 Friedrich Karl von Savigny
Savigny associated law with the historical consciousness and common life of a people — commonly called the **Volksgeist**. Law develops through custom and shared social practices, and later through the work of jurists and legal institutions. He opposed careless codification that ignores a society's legal development.

**Strengths:** draws attention to custom, continuity, social context and the risks of importing rules without understanding local institutions.

**Criticisms:** a society's “spirit” is hard to identify and may conceal disagreement; custom can preserve hierarchy or injustice; and waiting for organic development may delay necessary reform.

### 4.2 Sir Henry Sumner Maine
Maine is associated with the proposition that the movement of progressive societies has often been “from status to contract.” **Status** describes legal positions substantially fixed by birth, family or social position. **Contract** describes a greater role for individual agreement in organising social relations.

The proposition is a historical generalisation, not a universal law that every society always follows in one straight line. Modern societies also regulate contracts, protect vulnerable people and create new forms of status-based protection.

**Strengths:** connects law with social transformation and comparative history.

**Criticisms:** it can overgeneralise; contractual freedom is not equally available to everyone; and legal change can move toward more regulation as well as more individual choice.

### 4.3 Savigny and Maine compared
Savigny stresses the historical and cultural development of a people's law. Maine uses comparative legal history to identify broad patterns of institutional change. Both resist treating law as an entirely abstract set of commands separated from society.

## 5. Sociological School

Sociological jurisprudence studies law in relation to social needs, institutions and effects. It asks how law works in lived society and whether legal rules advance social order and human interests.

### 5.1 Roscoe Pound: social engineering
Pound described the law's task in terms of **social engineering**: identifying competing individual, public and social interests and working toward an order that satisfies as many of them as possible while minimising conflict. The judge or lawmaker should understand the practical effects of legal rules rather than treating law only as logic from fixed premises.

**Method for an answer**
1. Identify the social problem.
2. Identify competing interests.
3. Explain how rules or remedies balance them.
4. Consider practical consequences and unintended effects.
5. Evaluate whether the proposed balance is fair.

**Strengths:** makes law responsive to social needs and consequences.

**Criticisms:** there is no neutral mathematical formula for ranking interests; decision-makers may privilege powerful groups; and the language of “engineering” can understate rights and individual dignity.

### 5.2 Léon Duguit: social solidarity
Duguit challenged an absolute idea of state sovereignty and the idea that public authority exists simply because the State wills it. He emphasised social interdependence and **social solidarity**. Public power and institutions should be understood in light of the social functions they perform and their contribution to collective life.

**Strengths:** draws attention to social responsibility and the service obligations of public institutions.

**Criticisms:** the theory may understate the role of enforceable individual rights and leave questions about who defines the common social interest. Solidarity can be invoked by conflicting political programmes.

### 5.3 Pound and Duguit compared
Pound's focus is the balancing of interests through law; Duguit's central focus is social interdependence and the functional justification of public authority. Both reject an account of law that ignores society, but their concepts are not interchangeable.

## 6. American Modern Realism

### 6.1 Main idea
American legal realism examines how courts and other legal officials actually decide cases. It questions the assumption that every case outcome follows mechanically from clear rules. Facts, evidence, institutional habits, policy concerns, procedure and the characteristics of decision-makers can affect outcomes.

Writers associated with American realism include Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., Karl Llewellyn and Jerome Frank. They differed in emphasis; do not attribute every realist claim to every thinker.

### 6.2 Key propositions
- Written rules matter, but their application may be uncertain in difficult cases.
- A lawyer needs to understand how courts are likely to respond to facts and evidence.
- Empirical study of institutions and judicial practice can supplement doctrinal analysis.
- Predictions about what courts will do are one practical part of legal work, although a prediction alone does not settle what the law ought to be.

### 6.3 Contribution and criticism
Realism encourages attention to evidence, judicial reasoning, procedure and the real effect of law. Its limitations appear when a radical version reduces law to a prediction or treats rules as unimportant. Legal decisions remain constrained by statutes, precedents, jurisdiction and constitutional principles; realism is best used to explain uncertainty, not to claim that judges may decide anything.

## 7. Economic approach of law: Marxist approach

### 7.1 Main idea
Marxist approaches examine law in the context of economic organisation, class relations, property, production and power. In a classic Marxist framework, the material/economic structure of society strongly shapes political and legal institutions. Law can preserve existing relations of production and class power, even while presenting rules in universal and neutral language.

### 7.2 Questions asked by a Marxist analysis
- Who owns productive resources and who controls work?
- Which group benefits from a legal arrangement?
- Does a rule preserve or challenge existing economic power?
- How do property, contract, criminalisation and labour regulation relate to class structure?
- Does a formally equal rule produce unequal real outcomes?

### 7.3 Contribution and criticism
The approach highlights distribution, material power and the political economy of legal institutions. Critics argue that a crude version becomes economically reductionist: legal institutions may have relative autonomy, constitutional principles can restrain powerful actors, and law can also serve emancipatory reforms. Different Marxist thinkers disagree about how direct the relationship between economy and law is.

## 8. Revision: compare every school

| School / thinker | Core question | Central concept | Main criticism |

## 9. How to answer a 20/30-mark jurisprudence question

**Introduction:** define the school/theory and explain why it matters.  
**Body 1:** identify the jurist and explain the central concepts.  
**Body 2:** explain the theory's logic and provide an example.  
**Body 3:** give at least two considered criticisms.  
**Body 4:** compare with another school where relevant.  
**Conclusion:** state the theory's continuing value and limits.

Avoid these mistakes:
- Do not equate “law exists” with “law is just.”
- Do not say all positivists believe law is morally good.
- Do not treat Grundnorm as an ordinary enacted article.
- Do not attribute “status to contract” to Savigny; it is associated with Maine.
- Do not confuse Pound's social engineering with Duguit's social solidarity.
- Do not write that realism means rules never matter.
- Do not describe Marxist analysis only as “law is about money”; explain class and economic structure.
