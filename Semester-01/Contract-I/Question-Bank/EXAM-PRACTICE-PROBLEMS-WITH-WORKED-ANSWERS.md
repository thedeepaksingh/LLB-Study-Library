# K-1005 — Original Worked Exam Practice
**These are original hypothetical questions, not verified CCSU previous-year questions.**

## Part A — Long-answer structures

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-01. Explain proposal, acceptance and revocation.
Define proposal/promise; distinguish offer from invitation; explain absolute acceptance; set out communication/completion/revocation rules in sections 3–8; apply timing to a sequence of facts; conclude whether a contract arose.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-02. Explain consideration and the doctrine of privity.
Define consideration under section 2(d); explain that Indian law allows consideration to move from the promisee or another person; distinguish this from who may sue; discuss section 25 exceptions and recognised routes around strict privity.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-03. Discuss minor's agreements.
Apply sections 11–12, *Mohori Bibee*, and section 68 for necessaries; distinguish void contractual liability from reimbursement from property and avoid indirectly enforcing a minor's bargain.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-04. Distinguish coercion, undue influence, fraud and misrepresentation.
Set out sections 15–18 separately, then sections 19/19A consequences; focus on statutory ingredients and facts; explain voidability and any applicable exception.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-05. Explain lawful object and agreements declared void.
Discuss sections 23–30; distinguish object from consideration and void from illegal; explain relevant categories and exceptions.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-06. Explain standard-form contracts.
Discuss notice/incorporation, interpretation, exclusion clauses, unequal bargaining power, statutory consumer protection and limits; use *Central Inland Water Transport* in context.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-07. Explain contingent contracts.
Define under section 31; explain enforcement/non-happening/impossible-event rules under sections 32–36; distinguish from wagering and a contract with a mere future performance date.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-08. Explain quasi-contracts under sections 68–72.
Describe each covered situation and the prevention of unjust retention of benefits; make clear that the obligation arises by law, not actual agreement.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-09. Discuss frustration under section 56.
Explain initial impossibility and supervening impossibility/unlawfulness; distinguish frustration from hardship, breach and force-majeure clause; use *Satyabrata Ghose*.

### PRACTICE-CONTRACT-10. Explain compensation under sections 73–75.
Define breach, legally recoverable loss, remoteness, mitigation, stipulated sum, reasonable compensation and rightful rescission; use Indian statutory provisions.

## Part B — Worked problems

### Problem 1: Offer revoked
A emails B an offer to sell a laptop. Before B's acceptance reaches A, A communicates revocation in a legally effective way. B later sends an unconditional acceptance.
**Worked answer:** Determine how sections 3–6 apply to the medium and sequence. If revocation was effective before acceptance became complete against A, a contract may not arise. Do not assume an email's arrival, opening, sending or reading is always the legally decisive moment; apply the statutory rules and relevant electronic-communication law to the actual facts.

### Problem 2: Silent acceptance
A writes: “I will sell you my bicycle for ₹5,000. If I do not hear from you tonight, I will consider it sold.” B says nothing and does not act.
**Worked answer:** Silence alone ordinarily does not amount to acceptance simply because A declared it would. Unless some recognised rule or prior course of dealing changes the analysis, B has not signified assent. Consider section 7 and *Felthouse v Bindley* as an illustration.

### Problem 3: Reward unknowingly earned
A offers a reward for finding a missing dog. B finds the dog and returns it without knowing of the reward.
**Worked answer:** Knowledge of the offer is normally needed to accept it by performance. *Lalman Shukla v Gauri Dutt* is commonly used on this point. B's act may have helped A, but performance without knowledge is not necessarily acceptance of the reward offer.

### Problem 4: Minor's loan
A lender knowingly gives money on an ordinary loan agreement to a 16-year-old and later sues the minor personally for the contractual amount.
**Worked answer:** Apply sections 11–12 and *Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose*. The ordinary loan contract is generally void against the minor because the minor lacks contractual competence. A lender cannot automatically enforce the adult bargain by calling the claim restitution. Separate issues may arise concerning necessaries under section 68 or property-based relief, but ordinary personal contractual liability should not be assumed.

### Problem 5: Gift promise
A tells a friend, “I will gift you ₹50,000 next month,” but the friend gives no consideration and no statutory exception is established.
**Worked answer:** Under section 25, an agreement without consideration is generally void unless one of the specified exceptions applies. A gratuitous promise of a future gift is not enforceable merely because it was sincerely stated. Examine the exact statutory exception and formal requirements if additional facts are given.

### Problem 6: Consent obtained by threat
A threatens to unlawfully detain B's property unless B signs an agreement. B signs and later seeks to avoid it.
**Worked answer:** Test section 15's definition of coercion and whether it caused B's consent. If the statutory conditions are satisfied, section 19 may make the contract voidable at B's option. Do not simply write “the agreement is void”; identify the legal consequence and any question of rescission/restitution.

### Problem 7: Unconscionable standard term
An employer presents a standard document containing a clause allowing it to terminate the agreement arbitrarily. The employee has no meaningful bargaining power and the employer is a public corporation.
**Worked answer:** First interpret and establish incorporation of the clause. Then consider applicable statute, public-policy limits, unequal bargaining power and *Central Inland Water Transport Corp v Brojo Nath Ganguly* in its context. Do not infer that every standard-form contract is invalid; focus on the specific clause, parties and governing legal rules.

### Problem 8: Event-dependent promise
A promises to pay B ₹1 lakh if a specified ship returns to port within six months. The ship is delayed but has not yet sunk and the period has not elapsed.
**Worked answer:** This may be a contingent contract because the promise depends on a collateral event. Section 31 and the relevant rules in sections 32–36 control enforcement. Until the event occurs or the statutory time/impossibility condition applies, do not treat the condition as fulfilled. Compare the terms with a wagering agreement only if the facts support that separate classification.

### Problem 9: Public event becomes impossible
A venue is hired for a particular event. Before the event, a lawful government order makes use of that venue for the event impossible. The contract contains no clear risk-allocation clause in the stated facts.
**Worked answer:** Consider section 56 and *Satyabrata Ghose*. Determine whether the event makes the promised performance impossible or unlawful in the legal sense, whether the risk was allocated by the contract and whether the frustration test is met. Not every inconvenience, rise in cost or commercial disappointment frustrates a contract. More facts about the clause and purpose may be necessary.

### Problem 10: Lost expected profit
A supplier breaches by delivering late. The buyer claims both ordinary loss from the delay and extraordinary profits from a special resale contract that was never disclosed to the supplier.
**Worked answer:** Apply section 73. Identify direct/natural loss and assess whether the special loss was within the relevant contemplation/communication rules. Consider causation, remoteness and mitigation. The entire claimed amount is not automatically recoverable because breach occurred. Section 74 is relevant only if the contract contains a stipulated sum/penalty term in the circumstances described.

### Problem 11: Benefit without a valid contract
A public authority requests work from B. B lawfully performs it, does not intend it to be gratuitous, and the authority knowingly enjoys the benefit. The supposed contract cannot be enforced as a normal contract because a required formality was absent.
**Worked answer:** Consider section 70: lawful act/delivery, no intention to act gratuitously and enjoyment of benefit are key conditions. *State of West Bengal v B.K. Mondal & Sons* illustrates a section 70 obligation. The claim is statutory/quasi-contractual, not proof that the unenforceable contract becomes valid.
