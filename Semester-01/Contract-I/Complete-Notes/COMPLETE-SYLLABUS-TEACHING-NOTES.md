# Contract–I (K-1005): Complete Syllabus Teaching Notes
**General Principles of Contract · CCSU LL.B. Semester 1 · Beginner edition**

> Start with the Indian Contract Act, 1872. The case summaries in this pack are learning aids. Contract outcomes often turn on exact facts, statutory exceptions and whether a proposition comes from Indian law or a foreign decision.

## 1. Purpose, evolution and scope of contract law

Contract law governs promises/agreements that the law will enforce. It supports planned exchange: a buyer agrees to pay; a seller agrees to deliver; an employer and contractor allocate work; parties arrange time, risk and performance. Contract law can provide remedies when an enforceable promise is broken.

The law does not enforce every promise. Social promises may lack legal intention or certainty; agreements may lack consideration, capacity, consent or legality; and the Act declares some agreements void. Contract law balances freedom of agreement with restrictions protecting legal policy and vulnerable parties.

**Core concept:** an **agreement** is not automatically a **contract**. Section 2(h) defines contract as an agreement enforceable by law. Section 10 lays down the general requirements of enforceability, subject to the Act and other applicable law.

## 2. Offer, proposal, promise, acceptance and revocation

### 2.1 Proposal and promise
Section 2(a) defines a proposal through one person's signifying willingness to do or abstain from doing something, with a view to obtaining another person's assent. Section 2(b) addresses acceptance: when the person to whom a proposal is made signifies assent, the proposal is accepted and becomes a promise within the Act's definitions.

An offer must be sufficiently definite and communicated to the person who is to accept it. It must be distinguished from:
- **Invitation to offer:** invites others to make offers, e.g. many shop displays and advertisements, though the exact wording/circumstances matter.
- **Negotiation:** discussion that has not yet become a definite offer.
- **Statement of information/opinion:** does not necessarily promise a legal result.
- **Tender/auction announcement:** can invite offers; the governing terms decide when a binding offer/acceptance occurs.

### 2.2 Acceptance
Section 7 requires acceptance to be absolute and unqualified and made in the prescribed/usual and reasonable manner, subject to the section. A counter-offer ordinarily rejects or changes the original offer instead of accepting it as-is. Silence generally is not acceptance merely because the offeror says it will count as acceptance.

Section 8 recognises acceptance by performing the conditions of a proposal, or accepting consideration for a reciprocal promise, in the statutory context. A unilateral offer may be accepted by completing the specified act where the offer terms and law support that construction.

### 2.3 Communication and timing
Sections 3–6 deal with communication, completion and revocation of proposals/acceptances. Timing can decide whether the contract existed before a revocation, whether an offer lapsed, or where a contract was concluded. Apply the statutory rules to the communication method and factual sequence; do not use a single telephone/post rule without checking the applicable clause and authority.

For electronic communications, consider the Information Technology Act, 2000 and the governing contract principles. The place/time of contract formation can matter to jurisdiction, but the answer depends on the actual communications and applicable law.

### 2.4 Revocation
An offer can be revoked under the Act before acceptance becomes complete against the proposer in the relevant statutory sense, subject to the rules on communication and any valid option/irrevocability arrangement. Revocation can also occur by lapse of prescribed/reasonable time, failure of a condition precedent or death/insanity where the offeree learns the relevant fact before acceptance, as set out in section 6.

### 2.5 Case illustrations
- *Lalman Shukla v Gauri Dutt*: the claimant performed an act without knowing of the reward offer; knowledge of an offer is important to acceptance.
- *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co*: an advertisement may constitute a unilateral offer where its wording and circumstances show a sufficiently definite promise intended to be acted on.
- *Felthouse v Bindley*: the offeror cannot generally impose acceptance by silence alone.
- *Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v Girdharilal Parshottamdas*: communication/formation issues in contracts concluded by telephone; use the holding for its precise facts.

## 3. Consideration and privity of contract

### 3.1 Consideration — section 2(d)
Consideration is the act, abstinence or promise described in section 2(d), done at the desire of the promisor by the promisee or another person. It is the legal value supporting the promise. Under Indian law, consideration may move from the promisee **or any other person**, as the section states. Do not import the English rule on who must furnish consideration without noting this difference.

Consideration must be real in law but need not ordinarily be economically equal to the promise. Gross inadequacy can be evidence relevant to whether consent was free, but inadequate value by itself does not automatically make a contract void.

### 3.2 At the desire of the promisor
An act done voluntarily or at the request of a third person, rather than at the promisor's desire, may fail as consideration for that promisor's later promise, subject to statutory exceptions. *Durga Prasad v Baldeo* is commonly used to illustrate the desire-of-promisor requirement.

### 3.3 Past, present and future consideration
The Act's broad definition may recognise acts already done at the promisor's desire, current acts or promises of future performance. Explain the legal basis and timing; do not assume all past acts count as consideration.

### 3.4 Exceptions to “no consideration, no contract” — section 25
Section 25 states the general rule and specified exceptions. Commonly taught exceptions include:
1. An agreement made out of natural love and affection between parties in near relation, if the statutory formal requirements are met.
2. A promise to compensate wholly or partly a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor, or something the promisor was legally compellable to do, within the section.
3. A written and signed promise to pay wholly/partly a debt barred by limitation, within the section.
The section also contains explanations on adequacy and other details; consult its actual text before answering a precise problem. Gifts actually made are treated separately in the section.

### 3.5 Privity
The doctrine of privity generally means that a person who is not a party to a contract cannot ordinarily sue to enforce it merely because the contract was intended to benefit that person. Recognised Indian-law exceptions or routes can include trust/beneficiary arrangements, family arrangements, agency, assignment, acknowledgement/estoppel or statutory rights, depending on the facts. Do not memorise a supposed exception as universal.

**Important distinction:** consideration and privity are different issues. Under section 2(d), consideration may move from a person other than the promisee; that does not mean any stranger to the contract may sue.

## 4. Capacity to contract

### 4.1 Section 11 and sound mind
Section 11 says persons are competent if they have reached the age of majority according to the law applicable to them, are of sound mind, and are not disqualified from contracting by a law to which they are subject. Section 12 explains sound mind for contracting: the person must be able to understand the contract and form a rational judgment about its effect on their interests at the relevant time.

Capacity is assessed at the time the contract is made. A person with a condition or disability is not automatically incapable in every transaction; the statutory test and evidence matter.

### 4.2 Minor's agreements
In *Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose* (1903), the Privy Council treated the minor's agreement in issue as void. The usual teaching rule is that a minor is not competent under section 11 and a minor's agreement is generally void, rather than simply voidable at the minor's option.

Consequences:
- Do not assume the minor is personally bound by an ordinary contractual promise.
- Section 68 permits reimbursement from the incapable person's property for necessaries supplied to them or someone they are legally bound to support; this is not the same as ordinary personal liability on a contract.
- Fraudulent misrepresentation of age does not automatically turn a minor's agreement into an enforceable adult contract; seek the exact Indian rule and remedy.
- Restitution is fact- and remedy-sensitive. It should not be used to indirectly enforce a bargain the law treats as void against the minor.

### 4.3 Persons disqualified by law
Some persons may be subject to legal disabilities or special restrictions in particular contexts. Do not list a category as universally incapable without checking the current statute and facts. State the legal source and the particular restriction.

## 5. Consent and free consent

### 5.1 Consent — section 13
Consent exists where parties agree upon the same thing in the same sense (consensus ad idem). If they are genuinely talking about different subjects or meanings, valid consent may be missing.

### 5.2 Free consent — section 14
Consent is free when it is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake as described by the Act. Each vitiating factor has its own legal definition and consequence.

### 5.3 Coercion — section 15
Coercion concerns committing or threatening an act forbidden by criminal law, or unlawfully detaining/threatening to detain property, with the intent of causing a person to enter into an agreement, as the section provides. Use the statutory definition; ordinary persuasion or commercial pressure is not automatically coercion.

### 5.4 Undue influence — section 16
Undue influence arises where the relationship between parties is such that one is in a position to dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage. The section identifies circumstances relevant to domination, including certain authority, fiduciary or dependency relationships. A court may examine burden of proof and whether the transaction appears unconscionable under the statutory framework.

### 5.5 Fraud — section 17
Fraud includes specified acts such as suggesting as fact something untrue by a person who does not believe it true, active concealment, a promise made without intention of performing it, and other acts covered by the section. Mere silence is not automatically fraud, except where there is a duty to speak or silence is equivalent to speech in the legally defined circumstances.

### 5.6 Misrepresentation — section 18
Misrepresentation concerns specified false assertions or conduct that misleads without the fraudulent intent required for fraud, or other categories defined in section 18. Distinguish a careless/innocent misstatement from deliberate deception. Consequences and exceptions may differ.

### 5.7 Consequence — sections 19 and 19A
A contract caused by coercion, fraud or misrepresentation may be voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused, subject to statutory requirements and exceptions. Undue influence is addressed through section 19A, including the court's power in appropriate cases. Do not write that every defect of consent automatically makes the contract void from the beginning.

### 5.8 Mistake — sections 20–22
- **Bilateral mistake of essential fact (section 20):** where both parties are under a mistake of fact essential to the agreement, the agreement may be void as described by the section.
- **Mistake of Indian law (section 21):** does not ordinarily make a contract voidable on that ground; the Act distinguishes foreign law in the provision.
- **Unilateral mistake of fact (section 22):** a contract is not voidable merely because one party was mistaken as to a fact, subject to the text and other doctrines.
Mistake must be about the relevant kind of fact and sufficiently fundamental under the provision; a bad bargain or misunderstanding is not automatically a section 20 mistake.

## 6. Lawful consideration and object; void agreements

### 6.1 Section 23
Consideration or object is unlawful where it falls within the statutory categories, including being forbidden by law, defeating the provisions of law, fraudulent, involving injury to person/property, or regarded as immoral or opposed to public policy under the provision.

Differentiate **object** (purpose) from **consideration** (value/act/promise supporting the bargain). If the unlawful element cannot be separated where section 24 applies, the agreement may be void.

### 6.2 Common void-agreement categories
- **No consideration:** section 25, subject to statutory exceptions.
- **Restraint of marriage:** section 26.
- **Restraint of trade:** section 27, subject to applicable statutory exceptions; do not assume every commercial restriction is valid just because it is labelled a confidentiality clause.
- **Restraint of legal proceedings:** section 28 and its exceptions.
- **Uncertainty:** section 29; the term must be sufficiently certain or capable of being made certain.
- **Wagering agreements:** section 30; apply statutory terms and separate wagering from insurance, contingent contracts and other transactions where legally relevant.
- **Unlawful consideration/object:** sections 23–24.
- **Void because mutual mistake of essential fact:** section 20.
“Void” means not enforceable as a contract. “Illegal” may have wider consequences for related transactions; do not use the words as exact synonyms in every case.

### 6.3 Void, voidable and unenforceable
- **Void agreement:** the Act treats it as not enforceable as a contract.
- **Voidable contract:** one party has an option to avoid it under the law, e.g. in specified free-consent situations.
- **Unenforceable:** a transaction may have an evidentiary/procedural defect preventing enforcement, even if the underlying agreement is not declared void; explain the specific reason.
- **Illegal:** the object/consideration violates a legal prohibition or public policy category; consequences can extend beyond mere non-enforcement.

## 7. Standard-form contracts
A standard-form contract is drafted in advance by one party for repeated use, often with little genuine negotiation by the other party. Examples include online terms, insurance documents, transport tickets and service-provider terms.

Issues include:
- whether the terms were incorporated and brought to the other party's notice;
- whether an unusual/exclusion clause received sufficient notice;
- interpretation of ambiguity;
- statutory controls and consumer protection;
- unconscionability/unequal bargaining power in legally relevant settings;
- whether one party consented or was practically forced to accept a form.

Not every standard term is invalid. A court first asks what was incorporated and how the term should be interpreted, then considers any recognised statutory or public-policy limitation. *Central Inland Water Transport Corporation v Brojo Nath Ganguly* is often discussed for unconscionable terms and unequal bargaining power in its particular public-employment context.

## 8. Contingent contracts — sections 31–36
Section 31 defines a contingent contract as a contract to do or not do something if some event collateral to the contract does or does not happen. Sections 32–36 address enforcement based on happening/non-happening, impossible events and time-bound conditions.

A contingent contract is not simply any contract with a future due date. The specified event must be collateral in the statutory sense. Ask:
1. What is the event?
2. Is it collateral to the promise?
3. Does the contract depend on the event happening or not happening?
4. Has the event happened, become impossible or failed within the specified time?
5. Which section controls enforcement?

Do not confuse a contingent contract with a wagering agreement; the latter is treated separately under section 30, subject to statutory distinctions.

## 9. Quasi-contracts — sections 68–72
Quasi-contract is the common name for certain statutory obligations where the law imposes a duty resembling a contractual obligation despite the absence of a normal express agreement. The aim is to prevent unjust retention of benefits within the Act's categories.

- **Section 68:** necessaries supplied to a person incapable of contracting or someone that person is legally bound to support; reimbursement from property.
- **Section 69:** a person interested in paying money that another is legally bound to pay may, in the section's circumstances, be entitled to reimbursement.
- **Section 70:** where a person lawfully does something for another or delivers something, not intending it gratuitously, and the other enjoys the benefit, compensation/restoration may be required.
- **Section 71:** finder of goods has responsibilities resembling those of a bailee.
- **Section 72:** money/thing delivered by mistake or under coercion must be repaid/returned under the section.

A quasi-contract is not a fictional agreement inferred because the parties must have intended it. The obligation arises by law when the statutory conditions are met.

## 10. Discharge of contract and frustration

### 10.1 Modes of discharge
A contract may be discharged by:
- performance or valid tender of performance (sections 37–38);
- agreement, including novation, rescission or alteration (section 62);
- remission/dispensation by the promisee (section 63);
- breach/repudiation in the statutory circumstances (section 39 and other rules);
- impossibility/frustration (section 56);
- operation of law, where applicable.

The exact rule depends on the type of promise and the event. A party cannot assume that an informal change cancels the original obligations unless the law recognises it.

### 10.2 Section 56: impossibility and frustration
The first part of section 56 addresses an agreement to do an act impossible in itself. The later part addresses a contract to do an act that, after formation, becomes impossible or unlawful because of an event the statute covers; the contract becomes void when the act becomes impossible/unlawful in that sense.

**Frustration** is not a general excuse because performance becomes harder or less profitable. Ask whether the event fundamentally alters the obligation as the law requires, whether the contract allocated that risk, whether performance remains possible in a legally relevant sense, and whether a force-majeure clause governs. The clause and section 56 must be interpreted carefully together.

* Satyabrata Ghose v Mugneeram Bangur & Co.* is a leading Indian case on section 56; it explains that impossibility is not always literal physical impossibility, but the doctrine remains controlled by the contract and circumstances.

### 10.3 Novation and alteration
Section 62 deals with novation, rescission and alteration by agreement. Novation substitutes a new contract for the old one with the required consent; rescission cancels; alteration changes terms. Do not call every variation a novation.

## 11. Remedies: compensation for breach

### 11.1 Sections 73–75
Section 73 addresses compensation for loss or damage caused by breach, subject to the statute and remoteness principles. It includes loss that naturally arose in the usual course or that the parties knew to be likely when contracting; remote and indirect losses are not treated the same as recoverable loss under the provision. Mitigation and causation matter.

Section 74 addresses compensation where a sum is named or a penalty stipulation exists, with the statutory rule of reasonable compensation not exceeding the stipulated amount in the circumstances covered. The amount written in the contract is not automatically recoverable in full regardless of loss or law.

Section 75 concerns a party who rightfully rescinds being entitled to compensation for damage sustained through non-fulfilment, under the statutory conditions.

### 11.2 Damages framework
Ask:
1. What was the contractual promise?
2. Was there breach and what caused it?
3. What loss was suffered?
4. Is the loss within the relevant remoteness rule?
5. Could the loss reasonably have been mitigated?
6. Is there a valid exclusion/liquidated-damages term and how does law treat it?
7. What remedy is legally available?

In *Hadley v Baxendale* (English authority used for the remoteness principle), recoverability depends on the kind of loss that arises naturally or was within the parties' contemplation under the relevant rule. Use Indian statutory section 73 and Indian authorities where relevant.

## 12. Syllabus quick comparisons

### Offer v invitation to offer
A definite offer gives the other party power to accept it; an invitation invites the other person to make an offer. Wording, context and surrounding conduct determine classification.

### Coercion v undue influence v fraud v misrepresentation
- Coercion: threat/act of the statutory kind used to cause agreement.
- Undue influence: position to dominate will plus unfair advantage.
- Fraud: statutory deception category with the required intention/conduct.
- Misrepresentation: statutory false statement/conduct without every fraud element.
Each has distinct elements and consequences.

### Void v voidable
Void: not enforceable as a contract under the relevant rule. Voidable: enforceable unless/until the entitled party avoids it under the law. State the statutory basis and whether the affected party exercised the option.

### Frustration v breach
Frustration under section 56 may discharge an obligation due to a qualifying supervening event; breach is failure/refusal to perform an obligation that remains binding. A party cannot manufacture frustration by its own conduct.

## 13. Exam answer structure
**Rule:** cite section. **Elements:** unpack each statutory requirement. **Application:** link the facts to each element. **Exception:** assess it separately. **Effect:** void/voidable/enforceable/discharged/breached. **Remedy:** compensation or statutory restitution where appropriate. **Conclusion:** state the likely result and missing facts.
