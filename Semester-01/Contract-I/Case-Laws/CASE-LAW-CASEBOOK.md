# K-1005 — Contract–I Case-Law Casebook
**Educational summaries. Check the report and current treatment before formal citation. Foreign decisions are labelled as such.**

## Offer, acceptance and communication

### 1. Lalman Shukla v Gauri Dutt (1913)
**Facts:** An employee carried out a task before learning that a reward had been offered for finding the missing person.  
**Principle:** A person cannot ordinarily accept an offer of which they were unaware at the time of performing the requested act.  
**Use:** Offer knowledge and acceptance; distinguish reward cases from an act performed in reliance on a known offer.

### 2. Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co (1893, English)
**Facts:** The company advertised a reward to a person who used its product in the specified way and still contracted influenza, and referred to a deposit as evidence of seriousness.  
**Principle:** On its wording and circumstances, the advertisement could be a unilateral offer accepted by performing the conditions, without prior individual notification.  
**Use:** Advertisements and unilateral contracts. This is a foreign decision; apply Indian statute and facts.

### 3. Felthouse v Bindley (1862, English)
**Facts:** An uncle wrote that unless he heard otherwise he would consider a horse sold to him; the nephew did not communicate acceptance.  
**Principle:** An offeror cannot generally stipulate that silence alone counts as acceptance.  
**Use:** Acceptance must be communicated or otherwise satisfy a recognised legal rule.

### 4. Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v Girdharilal Parshottamdas (1966)
**Issue:** Where was a contract formed through telephone communications?  
**Principle:** The Supreme Court considered when acceptance communicated by telephone completes a contract; the case is used for the place-of-contract rule in instantaneous communications.  
**Use:** Communication, acceptance and jurisdiction. Quote the precise rule from the judgment rather than reducing every electronic communication to the same result.

## Consideration, capacity and privity

### 5. Durga Prasad v Baldeo (1880)
**Facts/principle:** The case is used for the requirement that consideration must be done at the desire of the promisor. An act performed at another's request does not automatically constitute consideration for a later promise by the promisor.  
**Use:** Section 2(d), source/purpose of the act and consideration.

### 6. Chinnaya v Ramayya (1882)
**Issue:** Whether consideration can move from a person other than the promisee under Indian contract law.  
**Principle:** The case is conventionally used to illustrate the difference between Indian section 2(d)'s wording and the stricter English approach to consideration moving from the promisee.  
**Use:** Consideration v privity. Do not infer that every stranger to the contract may sue.

### 7. Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose (1903)
**Facts:** A minor executed a mortgage-related transaction; capacity and enforceability were disputed.  
**Principle:** The minor's agreement in issue was treated as void; a minor is not competent to contract under section 11.  
**Use:** Capacity and minor's agreements. Do not automatically extend ordinary adult contractual liability to a minor.

### 8. Kedar Nath Bhattacharji v Gorie Mohamed (1886)
**Facts/principle:** A promise to contribute to a public-purpose fund became supported by consideration where the promisee incurred liability relying on it in the relevant circumstances.  
**Use:** Section 25 exception for compensation for something done or liability incurred, subject to exact facts and statutory test.

## Consent and standard-form terms

### 9. Derry v Peek (1889, English)
**Issue:** Whether a misleading statement in a prospectus was fraudulent.  
**Principle:** The traditional fraud test required an untrue statement made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly as to truth/falsity; use current Indian section 17 and authority for an Indian statutory answer.  
**Use:** Fraud v innocent or negligent misrepresentation. Clearly label it as English authority.

### 10. Central Inland Water Transport Corporation Ltd v Brojo Nath Ganguly (1986)
**Issue:** Validity of a one-sided contractual term in a public-sector employment setting with unequal bargaining power.  
**Principle:** The Supreme Court invalidated the impugned unconscionable term in the context of the case and examined public policy and unequal bargaining power.  
**Use:** Standard-form contracts and public policy. Do not claim every non-negotiated term is void.

## Frustration, restitution and damages

### 11. Satyabrata Ghose v Mugneeram Bangur & Co (1954)
**Issue:** How section 56 applies where later events affect performance.  
**Principle:** “Impossible” in section 56 is not limited to literal physical impossibility, but frustration remains controlled by the contract and legal test; increased difficulty or inconvenience alone is not automatically enough.  
**Use:** Frustration, supervening impossibility and contract allocation of risk.

### 12. State of West Bengal v B.K. Mondal & Sons (1962)
**Issue:** Compensation for work/benefit under section 70 where the arrangement was not enforceable as a normal contract.  
**Principle:** The case is a leading illustration of statutory obligation where a person lawfully does something, not gratuitously, and the other enjoys the benefit within section 70.  
**Use:** Quasi-contracts; identify all section 70 conditions.

### 13. Hadley v Baxendale (1854, English)
**Facts:** Delay in delivery of a broken mill shaft caused business losses, and the parties disputed the extent of recoverable losses.  
**Principle:** The classic remoteness approach distinguishes losses arising naturally from those depending on special circumstances communicated or within contemplation.  
**Use:** Read alongside Indian Contract Act section 73 and Indian authorities; do not treat the English decision as the statutory wording.

### 14. Fateh Chand v Balkishan Das (1963)
**Issue:** Forfeiture/compensation clause and the statutory principle governing stipulated sums.  
**Principle:** The Supreme Court discussed section 74 and the court's power to award reasonable compensation subject to the statute, rather than automatically enforcing a penalty sum.  
**Use:** Liquidated damages/penalty and section 74; check the contract and applicable law.

## How to use cases in a contract answer
Cite a case only after stating the rule it illustrates. Then apply section text and facts. Cases about offer, consent, capacity and frustration address different legal questions; do not use one as a substitute for analysing all elements.
