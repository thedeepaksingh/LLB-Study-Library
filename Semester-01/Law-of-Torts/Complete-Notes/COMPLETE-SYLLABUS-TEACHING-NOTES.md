# Law of Torts & Consumer Protection Law (K-1003): Complete Teaching Notes
**CCSU LL.B. Semester 1 · Beginner edition · Simple English**

> This is an expanded study draft built around the syllabus topics. Tort rules and defences are fact-sensitive; case summaries should be checked against the reports and current legislation before formal citation.

## 1. Meaning, nature and ingredients of tort

### 1.1 What is a tort?
A tort is a civil wrong for which the law provides a remedy, commonly damages. Tort duties are generally imposed by law rather than created only by agreement between the parties. A tort claim is different from a criminal prosecution, although the same conduct can sometimes give rise to both civil and criminal proceedings.

A useful working definition is: **a breach of a duty fixed by law that causes a legally recognised injury and for which civil law provides a remedy**. It is a working description, not a single exhaustive statutory definition.

### 1.2 Tort compared with contract and crime
- **Tort:** duty generally arises from law; remedy often damages/injunction; claimant normally proves a civil claim.
- **Contract:** duty arises from an agreement enforceable by law; claimant must establish the contract and breach.
- **Crime:** public wrong prosecuted under criminal law; guilt and punishment are determined under criminal procedure and the applicable proof standard.

A single event may be both a tort and a crime. For example, a deliberate assault may support a criminal charge and a civil damages claim. The elements and procedure are not interchangeable.

### 1.3 Core constituents
For many torts, identify:
1. A legal right or protected interest.
2. A duty recognised by law.
3. Breach of that duty (or a legally actionable invasion of the right).
4. Causation linking the conduct to injury.
5. Damage where the particular tort requires proof of damage.
6. No complete defence that defeats the claim.

The details differ. Negligence usually requires proof of damage; some direct interferences with rights can be actionable without proof of substantial actual loss.

### 1.4 Damnum sine injuria and injuria sine damnum
**Damnum sine injuria** means loss or harm without violation of a legal right. The law does not compensate every disadvantage. A lawful competitor may cause economic loss by operating a better business.

**Injuria sine damnum** means violation of a legal right without measurable damage. Certain rights-based torts may support nominal damages because the right itself was infringed.

Cases traditionally associated with the distinction include *Gloucester Grammar School Case* for lawful competition causing loss, and *Ashby v White* for interference with a voting right despite no proven financial loss.

Do not reverse the Latin expressions:
- *Damnum* = loss/damage.
- *Injuria* = legal injury/violation of a right, not merely physical injury.

### 1.5 Mental element: malice, motive and negligence
- **Intention:** deliberate conduct or result, depending on the tort.
- **Malice in fact:** actual ill will or improper motive.
- **Malice in law:** conduct without lawful justification in a legal context where the term is relevant; personal spite is not always necessary.
- **Motive:** why the person acted. A bad motive does not automatically make lawful conduct tortious, and a good motive does not excuse every unlawful act.
- **Negligence:** failure to take the care the law requires, assessed against the applicable standard.

The mental element varies by tort. Defamation, deceit, malicious prosecution and nuisance do not use exactly the same mental test.

## 2. Who may sue and who may be sued?

### 2.1 Claimant
Normally the person whose legally protected interest is infringed may sue. Depending on the tort, legal representatives may pursue a claim or continue litigation where statute/common law permits. A claim may be restricted by the nature of the injury (for example, some personal claims traditionally do not survive in the same form) and by statutory rules.

A corporation may sue for injury to its business reputation in appropriate circumstances, but it cannot suffer bodily pain as a natural person does. Standing must be connected to the particular right and remedy.

### 2.2 Defendant
A person or legal entity responsible for the tort can be sued subject to capacity, immunity, statutory protection and procedural rules. Employers may sometimes be vicariously liable for employee torts. Public authorities can be liable, but the correct rule depends on the nature of the act, the governing statute and constitutional jurisprudence.

### 2.3 State liability and sovereign immunity
Older Indian case law distinguished sovereign and non-sovereign functions. *Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v State of Uttar Pradesh* (1965) is associated with a restrictive approach to State liability for a loss connected with police functions. *State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati* (1962) accepted State liability for a negligent act in a non-sovereign context. Later decisions, including *N. Nagendra Rao & Co. v State of Andhra Pradesh* (1994), narrowed blanket reliance on sovereign immunity and stressed accountability.

**Exam approach:** describe the older distinction, explain the cases, and then state that public-law constitutional compensation may be a distinct remedy for violation of fundamental rights. Do not present sovereign immunity as an automatic defence for every government act or assume every injury caused by a State employee yields damages.

## 3. Strict liability and absolute liability

### 3.1 Strict liability — Rylands v Fletcher
The classic rule concerns the escape of a dangerous thing brought onto land for a non-natural or special use, causing damage outside the land. The traditional elements are often summarised as:
1. The defendant brings/accumulates something likely to cause mischief if it escapes.
2. The use of the land is non-natural/special in the legally relevant sense.
3. The thing escapes from the defendant's land/control.
4. The escape causes the relevant damage.

Traditional exceptions/defences may include the claimant's own default, consent, an independent third party's act, act of God and statutory authority, subject to the relevant doctrine and facts.

The rule is not simply “any dangerous activity causes strict liability”. The required relationship to land, escape and legally recognised risk matters.

### 3.2 Absolute liability in India
In *M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (Oleum Gas Leak case, 1987), the Supreme Court developed a more stringent rule for enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities. Such an enterprise owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community; the rule is not subject to the traditional *Rylands* exceptions in the same way. Compensation was linked to the magnitude and capacity of the enterprise in the case's formulation.

| Point | Traditional strict liability | Indian absolute liability for hazardous enterprise |
|---|---|---|
| Core setting | Escape of a dangerous thing from land, under *Rylands* conditions | Hazardous/inherently dangerous enterprise |
| Exceptions | Traditional defences may apply | Traditional *Rylands* exceptions are not imported as general defences |
| Foundation | Older common-law rule | Indian Supreme Court's rule in *M.C. Mehta* |
| Exam method | Prove land/thing/escape/damage and test defences | Establish enterprise/activity and the governing absolute-duty principle |

## 4. Justifications and defences in tort

### 4.1 Volenti non fit injuria
The maxim means that a person who freely and knowingly consents to a risk may be unable to complain of the injury within the scope of that consent. Mere awareness of a risk is not always voluntary consent. Consent must be real and legally effective, and the defendant cannot assume that a claimant agreed to negligent conduct simply because the claimant entered a risky place or occupation.

Ask:
- Did the claimant know the nature and extent of the risk?
- Did the claimant freely consent to accept it?
- Was the injury within the scope of the accepted risk?
- Does statute or public policy limit the defence?

### 4.2 Necessity
An act may be justified where it is reasonably necessary in the circumstances to prevent a greater or imminent harm. The legal requirements are context-specific. Necessity is not a blank cheque to interfere with another's rights simply because the defendant believes their objective is beneficial.

### 4.3 Inevitable accident
An inevitable accident is an event not avoidable despite reasonable care in the relevant circumstances. If the harm occurred because the defendant failed to take required care, the defence is not established merely by calling the event an accident.

### 4.4 Act of God
An Act of God traditionally means damage caused exclusively by extraordinary natural forces that could not reasonably have been anticipated or prevented under the applicable test. Ordinary seasonal rain or a predictable natural event is not automatically an Act of God. Human contribution can defeat the defence.

### 4.5 Statutory authority
Where an Act authorises a particular activity, a defendant may have a defence to consequences that are necessarily incident to properly exercising that authority, subject to statutory conditions. A defendant normally cannot rely on authority to excuse negligence where the law requires reasonable care or where the conduct exceeds the authority.

### 4.6 Other relevant defences
Depending on the tort: claimant's consent, contributory negligence, lawful justification, limitation, privilege, truth/justification in defamation, and statutory defences. Do not list a defence without testing its ingredients against the alleged tort.

## 5. Liability for torts committed by others

### 5.1 Vicarious liability
Vicarious liability makes one person responsible for another's tort because of a legally relevant relationship, commonly employer and employee. It is not based simply on ownership of the workplace. The core questions are:
1. Was the tortfeasor an employee or in another relationship giving rise to vicarious liability?
2. Was the wrongful act sufficiently connected to the course/scope of employment?
3. Was it authorised, an improper mode of doing authorised work, or otherwise sufficiently connected under the governing test?

An employer is generally not automatically vicariously liable for every wrong by an independent contractor, although non-delegable duties and other exceptions can matter. Personal frolic or a purely private act may fall outside employment; a wrongful method of carrying out assigned work may fall within it.

### 5.2 Joint tortfeasors
Two or more persons may be jointly or independently responsible for the same harm. Examples include acting together pursuant to a common design, authorising another's tort, or separate torts that combine to cause indivisible damage. Analyse each party's conduct and legal connection rather than saying every person present shares liability.

### 5.3 State and public-authority liability
Start with the claimant's cause of action: ordinary negligence, vicarious liability, statutory compensation or a constitutional/public-law remedy. The nature of the function and the governing precedent can affect the outcome. Public-law compensation for a clear fundamental-rights violation is not identical to an ordinary damages action in tort.

## 6. Specific tort: negligence

### 6.1 Elements
Negligence is not merely carelessness in ordinary speech. A claimant generally proves:
1. **Duty of care:** the defendant owed a legal duty in the relationship/circumstances.
2. **Breach:** the defendant failed to meet the standard of reasonable care.
3. **Factual causation:** the breach caused the harm under the applicable causal test.
4. **Remoteness/legal causation:** the kind of harm is sufficiently connected/foreseeable under the governing rule.
5. **Damage:** a legally recognised loss occurred.

### 6.2 Duty and standard of care
The standard is ordinarily that of a reasonable person in the circumstances, adjusted where legally relevant for professional skill or the activity's risk. The duty question is distinct from whether the defendant breached it. The fact that a bad outcome occurred does not automatically prove negligence.

### 6.3 Causation and remoteness
Ask whether the injury would have occurred but for the breach, while recognising that difficult multiple-cause cases require more careful legal analysis. Then ask whether the damage is too remote. Avoid claiming that every consequence flowing historically from an act is legally recoverable.

### 6.4 Case principle
In *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932), a consumer alleged illness after finding a decomposed snail in a sealed bottle of ginger beer. The case is famous for the neighbour principle and the modern development of duties of care to persons closely and directly affected by one's acts. Apply the underlying duty reasoning to the problem rather than simply writing “manufacturer is always liable”.

## 7. Specific tort: nuisance

### 7.1 Private nuisance
Private nuisance is a substantial and unreasonable interference with another person's use or enjoyment of land or with a land-related interest. Factors can include duration, locality, intensity, timing and the character of the interference. Not every annoyance is actionable.

Examples can include recurring excessive noise, smoke, vibrations, smell or encroaching water, depending on context. The claimant's interest in the affected land and the actual interference matter.

### 7.2 Public nuisance
Public nuisance affects the public or a class of the public in a way recognised by law. Individual civil action may require special damage beyond the general public harm, subject to statutory routes and procedural rules. Criminal/statutory proceedings may be separate.

### 7.3 Remedies
Potential remedies include damages and injunction, but the choice depends on continuing harm, the balance of hardship, legal entitlement and statutory context. A court may shape injunctive relief to the proven wrong.

## 8. Specific tort: trespass

### 8.1 Trespass to land
Trespass to land generally involves a direct, unjustified physical intrusion onto land in another's possession. The claimant need not always prove substantial financial loss to establish the interference. Entry without permission, remaining after permission ends, or direct placement of an object can raise trespass issues depending on facts and law.

Defences may include consent/licence, lawful authority or other legal justification. Do not confuse trespass with nuisance: trespass focuses on direct intrusion; nuisance usually focuses on interference with use/enjoyment.

### 8.2 Trespass to the person
The related torts can include assault (causing reasonable apprehension of imminent unlawful force), battery (intentional/direct application of unlawful force), and false imprisonment (total restraint of liberty without lawful justification). The precise elements and defences vary. A threat at a distance, an actual contact and confinement are legally distinct types of conduct.

## 9. Specific tort: defamation

Defamation protects reputation against legally actionable publication of a defamatory statement referring to the claimant. A typical analysis asks:
1. Does the statement carry a defamatory meaning under the applicable test?
2. Does it refer to the claimant, expressly or by implication?
3. Was it communicated/published to at least one third person?
4. Are required fault rules met in the applicable civil/criminal context?
5. Does a defence apply?

Potential defences include truth/justification, honest opinion/fair comment within the governing doctrine, privilege, consent and other recognised grounds. The rules differ between civil tort claims and criminal defamation, so do not combine their elements.

“Libel” and “slander” traditionally distinguish forms of publication, but be careful applying old English categories mechanically in Indian law. Context, words/images, audience, reputation, and the relevant legal cause of action matter. Criticism of a person is not automatically actionable; nor does calling a statement “opinion” always protect it.

## 10. Consumer Protection Act, 2019

### 10.1 Purpose and broad framework
The Act provides a statutory system to protect consumers and redress consumer disputes. It deals with consumer rights, unfair trade practices, product liability, consumer commissions and mediation among other matters. Use the enacted Act and current rules; the Act is not merely an older common-law tort action.

### 10.2 Consumer — section 2(7)
The statutory definition broadly covers a person who buys goods or hires/avails services for consideration, including certain approved users/beneficiaries. It generally excludes purchases for resale or commercial purpose, subject to the Act's explanation, including the self-employment livelihood qualification. The facts and wording of the definition matter: not every purchaser or business user automatically qualifies.

### 10.3 Goods, service, defect and deficiency
- **Defect (section 2(10)):** a fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard required by law/contract/representation, subject to the statutory definition.
- **Deficiency (section 2(11)):** a fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality/nature/manner of performance of a service as the Act provides; negligent acts/omissions and relevant withholding of information may be covered by the definition.
- **Unfair trade practice (section 2(47)):** specified unfair/deceptive practices, as defined by the Act.

A defective product and a deficient service are not identical. Identify whether the complaint concerns a good, service, deceptive trade practice, product liability or another statutory ground.

### 10.4 Contract of service v contract for services
This distinction often asks whether the relationship is one of employment/service under a contract of service or an independent service arrangement. The Consumer Protection Act's “service” definition excludes service rendered free of charge or under a contract of personal service. The Supreme Court's case law has addressed how payment, free service, insurance/medical arrangements and personal service affect consumer status. Analyse the actual arrangement, not just the label on the document.

### 10.5 Consumer rights and remedies
The Act recognises consumer rights including protection against hazardous goods/services, information, choice, being heard, redress and consumer awareness. Depending on jurisdiction and the proved complaint, a commission may order remedies such as repair/removal of defect, replacement, refund, compensation, discontinuance of unfair practices, product-liability relief or other relief allowed by the Act.

### 10.6 Product liability
Chapter VI (sections 82 onward) provides for product-liability actions in defined circumstances against manufacturers, product sellers or service providers. Liability depends on statutory grounds such as manufacturing/design defect, deviation from specifications, express warranty or inadequate instructions/warnings, subject to the Act's elements and defences. Do not assume every adverse product result creates automatic liability.

### 10.7 Which commission?
Under the Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction of the District Commission, the State Commission and the National Commission) Rules, 2021, the financial thresholds are based on the **value of goods or services paid as consideration**:
- District Commission: does not exceed ₹50 lakh.
- State Commission: exceeds ₹50 lakh but does not exceed ₹2 crore.
- National Commission: exceeds ₹2 crore.

Check current amendments and the proper territorial, pecuniary and subject-matter jurisdiction before relying on these figures. The statutory threshold is not automatically the amount of compensation requested.

### 10.8 Consumer complaint — issue checklist
1. Is the complainant a consumer under section 2(7)?
2. Is the grievance a recognised consumer dispute/complaint?
3. Is it about defect, deficiency, unfair trade practice, product liability or another statutory ground?
4. Are statutory exclusions/defences applicable?
5. Which commission has jurisdiction based on the current rules?
6. Is the complaint within the applicable limitation period, subject to condonation?
7. What evidence and remedy fit the complaint?

## 11. Syllabus-wide comparison tables

### 11.1 Damnum sine injuria v injuria sine damnum
| Concept | Meaning | Classic illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Damnum sine injuria | Loss without legal-right infringement | Lawful competition causing financial loss |
| Injuria sine damnum | Legal-right infringement without proven actual loss | Denial of a protected vote in *Ashby v White* |

### 11.2 Negligence v strict/absolute liability
| Issue | Negligence | Strict liability | Absolute liability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core inquiry | Duty, breach, causation and damage | *Rylands* requirements, escape and damage | Hazardous enterprise under *M.C. Mehta* principle |
| Need to prove carelessness? | Yes, breach is central | Not the same requirement | Not the same requirement |
| Defences | Depends on tort | Traditional strict-liability exceptions | Traditional *Rylands* exceptions generally not available |
| Key caution | Bad result is not proof alone | Not any dangerous activity | Must establish governing hazardous-enterprise principle |

### 11.3 Private nuisance v trespass
- Trespass focuses on direct interference with possession/entry.
- Private nuisance focuses on substantial and unreasonable interference with use/enjoyment of land.
- One event may raise both, but prove the elements separately.
