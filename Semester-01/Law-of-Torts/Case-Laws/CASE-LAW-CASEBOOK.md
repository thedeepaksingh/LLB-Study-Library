# K-1003 — Case-Law Casebook
**Every entry is an educational summary. Confirm the report, facts and later treatment before formal citation.**

## 1. Ashby v White (1703)
**Facts:** A qualified voter was prevented from voting by an officer, although the candidate he supported won and the voter could not show actual financial loss.  
**Principle:** Interference with a legal right can support a remedy even without measurable damage where the right is actionable.  
**Syllabus use:** *Injuria sine damnum*. Contrast with lawful harm without infringement.

## 2. Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410)
**Facts:** A schoolmaster set up a competing school, leading to loss for an existing school.  
**Principle:** Loss caused by lawful competition is not necessarily a tort if no legal right is infringed.  
**Syllabus use:** *Damnum sine injuria*. The rule does not protect conduct that independently violates a legal right.

## 3. Donoghue v Stevenson (1932)
**Facts:** A consumer alleged illness after drinking from a sealed bottle containing a decomposed snail.  
**Issue:** Could a manufacturer owe a duty of care to a person who was not the buyer and had no direct contract?  
**Principle:** The House of Lords developed the neighbour principle and recognised a duty of care in the circumstances.  
**Use:** Negligence, duty of care, product-related harm. Do not generalise that every manufacturer is liable regardless of causation or applicable law.

## 4. Rylands v Fletcher (1868)
**Facts:** Water accumulated in a reservoir escaped through old workings and flooded the claimant's mine.  
**Principle:** The case formulated strict liability for escape of a dangerous thing brought onto land for a non-natural/special use, subject to the rule's requirements and exceptions.  
**Use:** Strict liability. State the elements and defences; do not call it India's absolute-liability rule.

## 5. M.C. Mehta v Union of India (Oleum Gas Leak, 1987)
**Facts:** Oleum gas escaped from an industrial enterprise in Delhi, causing harm and raising the safety duties of hazardous industry.  
**Principle:** The Supreme Court articulated a stringent absolute-liability principle for enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities; the rule is not subject to the traditional *Rylands* exceptions in the same way.  
**Use:** Distinguish Indian absolute liability from traditional English strict liability. Explain the activity and the legal basis.

## 6. State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati (1962)
**Facts:** A government vehicle driven negligently by a State employee caused a fatal accident.  
**Principle:** The Court accepted State liability in the non-sovereign context before it.  
**Use:** State liability. Describe the factual/functional context rather than saying all State conduct is treated identically.

## 7. Kasturi Lal Ralia Ram Jain v State of Uttar Pradesh (1965)
**Facts:** Property seized by police was not returned after it was misappropriated by a police employee.  
**Principle:** The Court applied a restrictive sovereign-function immunity approach in the case's circumstances.  
**Use:** Historical sovereign-immunity doctrine; pair with later cases to show development.

## 8. N. Nagendra Rao & Co. v State of Andhra Pradesh (1994)
**Facts:** Goods seized by public authorities were not properly preserved/returned, and liability of the State was disputed.  
**Principle:** The Supreme Court narrowed broad reliance on sovereign immunity and emphasised accountability, while the applicable result depends on the governmental act and context.  
**Use:** Later development of State liability and the limits of immunity.

## 9. Lucknow Development Authority v M.K. Gupta (1994)
**Issue:** Whether failures by a development authority could fall within consumer-protection jurisdiction and permit compensation.  
**Principle:** The Supreme Court interpreted consumer protection to provide meaningful redress against deficient public services in the circumstances covered by the Act then in force.  
**Use:** Consumer protection, service/deficiency and public bodies. Check the precise statutory regime and facts in a current problem.

## 10. Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha (1995)
**Issue:** Whether specified medical services fall within consumer-protection law.  
**Principle:** The Court addressed the treatment of medical services under the former consumer statute, including the role of consideration and free-service arrangements.  
**Use:** Contract of service/contract for services and consumer status. Do not cite this as a substitute for checking the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and later authority.

## 11. Municipal Corporation of Delhi v Subhagwanti (1966)
**Facts:** A clock tower collapsed, causing deaths, and the condition/maintenance of the structure was in issue.  
**Principle:** The case is commonly used for negligence and res ipsa loquitur in a situation where the event and control of the structure supported an inference requiring explanation.  
**Use:** Negligence/evidence. Res ipsa does not automatically prove liability in every accident; assess control and whether the event ordinarily occurs without negligence.

## How to use the case
Name the legal proposition and connect it to facts. A case citation without a correctly stated principle is not enough. Check whether a precedent addresses tort law generally, an earlier consumer statute, or a special statutory regime before applying it.
