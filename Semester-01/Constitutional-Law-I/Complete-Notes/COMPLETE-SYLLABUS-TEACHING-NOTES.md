## 1. Nature of the Indian Constitution

### 1.1 What is a constitution?
A constitution is the basic legal framework that establishes public institutions, distributes government power, sets limits on public authority and protects rights. India's written Constitution is supreme: government organs must act within it, and courts can review laws and action for constitutional compliance.

### 1.2 Important characteristics
- **Written and detailed:** the Constitution sets out institutions, powers, rights and procedures.
- **Constitutional supremacy:** ordinary legislation and executive action are subject to the Constitution.
- **Parliamentary government:** the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the elected House; the President is the constitutional head at the Union level.
- **Federal division of powers:** legislative powers are allocated between Union and States, notably through the Seventh Schedule and Articles 245–246 and related provisions.
- **A mixture of flexible and entrenched amendment rules:** Article 368 governs constitutional amendment, subject to the basic-structure limitation.
- **Independent judiciary and judicial review:** courts interpret the Constitution and can invalidate inconsistent laws or action within their jurisdiction.
- **Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties:** rights protect important freedoms; Directive Principles guide governance; Duties express civic responsibilities.
- **Secular and democratic constitutional framework:** government must comply with constitutional equality, religious freedom and democratic processes.

### 1.3 Federal structure
Federalism divides governmental power between at least two levels of government whose authority is constitutionally organised. India's system combines federal features with centralising features.

**Federal features**
1. Written, supreme Constitution.
2. Division of legislative powers between Union and States.
3. Constitutionally established State governments.
4. An independent judiciary to resolve constitutional disputes.
5. A special constitutional amendment procedure for certain federal provisions.

**Centralising/unitary features**
1. Strong Union legislative, administrative and financial powers in several fields.
2. Single citizenship.
3. Integrated judiciary and All-India Services.
4. Union role during constitutional emergencies.
5. Parliament's powers relating to State boundaries/names and specified circumstances in which Parliament may legislate on State subjects.

It is inaccurate to say that India is either entirely unitary or identical to the United States. The answer should recognise the distribution of powers and the centralising features. In *S.R. Bommai v Union of India* (1994), the Supreme Court discussed federalism and secularism as basic-structure principles.

### 1.4 Form of government
India follows a **parliamentary system**. The executive is drawn from and responsible to the legislature. The President is the constitutional head of the Union; the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers exercise executive government under the Constitution. States have a corresponding Governor/Chief Minister structure, subject to constitutional differences.

**Parliamentary vs presidential model**
- Parliamentary: executive leadership depends on legislative confidence; political responsibility is continuous.
- Presidential: executive is institutionally separate from the legislature and usually has a fixed term.
- India: parliamentary at Union and State levels, with written constitutional limits and judicial review.

The Constitution creates a **republic**: the head of State is not a hereditary monarch. The Preamble describes India as sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic and republic; “socialist” and “secular” were inserted into the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment in 1976.

## 2. The Preamble

### 2.1 Key words
The Preamble begins “We, the People of India” and expresses the Constitution's source of authority and objectives:
- **Sovereign:** India is legally independent in its internal and external affairs.
- **Socialist:** the Constitution permits and directs a welfare orientation and social/economic justice; it does not prescribe one rigid economic policy.
- **Secular:** the State has no official religion and must act consistently with religious freedom and equality.
- **Democratic:** public authority is based on constitutional democratic institutions.
- **Republic:** the head of State is not hereditary.
- **Justice:** social, economic and political.
- **Liberty:** thought, expression, belief, faith and worship.
- **Equality:** status and opportunity.
- **Fraternity:** dignity of the individual and unity/integrity of the nation.

### 2.2 Legal significance
The Preamble helps interpret constitutional text and explains constitutional objectives. In *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973), the Court treated the Preamble as part of the Constitution and recognised the basic-structure limit on amendment. The Preamble is not ordinarily an independent source of enforceable rights by itself; connect a claim to an operative constitutional provision.

## 3. Fundamental Rights: scheme and enforcement

### 3.1 Where they are found
Fundamental Rights appear primarily in Part III, Articles 12–35. They are enforceable constitutional guarantees. Article 12 defines “State” for Part III and includes the Union and State governments/legislatures and specified local or other authorities. Article 13 addresses laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights.

### 3.2 Who can claim them?
The answer depends on the right's wording:
- Some rights extend to **all persons**, including Article 14 and Article 21.
- Some are expressly for **citizens**, including the freedoms listed in Article 19.
- Other rights apply according to their text and context; Article 29(1), for example, protects a section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture.
Do not write that every Fundamental Right is available only to citizens.

### 3.3 Against whom?
Many Fundamental Rights primarily restrain the State, as defined in Article 12. Certain provisions also prohibit conduct by private persons. Examples include Article 17's abolition of untouchability, Article 23's prohibition of trafficking and forced labour, Article 24's child-labour restriction and Article 15(2)'s specified public-access rule. The exact wording of each Article matters.

### 3.4 Suspension during emergencies: do not confuse two mechanisms
**Article 358** concerns Article 19 during a Proclamation of Emergency declared on the ground of war or external aggression. It does not apply in the same way to an emergency proclaimed only on the ground of armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment changed its scope and safeguards; the actual constitutional text controls.

**Article 359** permits a Presidential order, during an emergency, to suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights in the way stated in the order. Articles 20 and 21 are protected from being included in such an order after the 44th Amendment. Explain the distinction between a right itself and access to judicial enforcement of that right.

Do not write the outdated absolute sentence “all Fundamental Rights automatically stop during every emergency.”

## 4. Right to Equality: Articles 14–18

### 4.1 Article 14 — equality before law and equal protection
Article 14 protects any person, not just citizens. It combines equality before law with equal protection of the laws.

A traditional reasonable-classification analysis asks:
1. Is there an intelligible differentia separating the group from others?
2. Does that differentia have a rational relation to the object of the law?

Modern Article 14 review also addresses arbitrariness and constitutional fairness. *E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu* is associated with the idea that arbitrariness is antithetical to equality. State the legal test appropriate to the facts rather than reciting only the two-part classification formula.

### 4.2 Articles 15–16
- **Article 15:** prohibits specified discrimination by the State on grounds including religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. It also contains constitutional provisions permitting specified special measures.
- **Article 16:** equality of opportunity in public employment, subject to its clauses and constitutionally permitted reservations/conditions.

Do not say that every classification based on one of these grounds is automatically unconstitutional. Read the Article's clauses, exceptions and applicable precedent.

### 4.3 Articles 17–18
- **Article 17:** abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form; legislation gives effect to the prohibition.
- **Article 18:** addresses titles, with exceptions and detailed rules in the Article.

## 5. Article 19(1)(a): freedom of speech and expression

Article 19(1)(a) protects citizens' freedom of speech and expression. This can include expression through words, writing, publication, art and other forms of communication. The right is not unlimited.

Article 19(2) permits only the constitutionally listed categories of reasonable restrictions, including sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation and incitement to an offence. A restriction must fit the constitutional ground and legal test; public discomfort alone is not enough.

**Exam approach:** identify the expression, name the asserted restriction, identify the Article 19(2) ground, and assess whether the legal restriction is constitutionally justified. *Shreya Singhal v Union of India* is a leading decision on online speech and the limits of vague criminal restrictions; its actual statutory context must be described accurately.

## 6. Article 20: protection in respect of conviction for offences

Article 20 provides three distinct protections:
1. **Article 20(1) — ex post facto criminal laws:** a person cannot be convicted for an act that was not an offence when done, nor subjected to a greater penalty than applied when it was committed, subject to the constitutional wording.
2. **Article 20(2) — double jeopardy:** no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once in the sense protected by the Article. It is not identical to every procedural rule concerning a second trial.
3. **Article 20(3) — self-incrimination:** a person accused of an offence cannot be compelled to be a witness against himself. Do not automatically equate testimonial compulsion with every search, fingerprint, medical examination or physical sample; relevant doctrine is fact-sensitive.

A good answer explains each clause separately and gives a case only for the precise proposition it supports.

## 7. Article 21: life and personal liberty

Article 21 states that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. In *Maneka Gandhi v Union of India* (1978), the Supreme Court required that procedure affecting personal liberty be fair, just and reasonable and read Articles 14, 19 and 21 together.

The Court has interpreted “life” to include more than bare physical survival. Depending on the issue, case law has connected Article 21 with dignity, privacy, livelihood, legal aid, fair procedure and other interests. Do not present every desirable social policy as an unlimited freestanding right: identify the specific doctrine and precedent.

### Privacy and dignity
The nine-judge bench in *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India* (2017) recognised privacy as a fundamental right. Restrictions on fundamental rights require a proper legal and constitutional analysis, including the relevant test articulated by the applicable judgments.

### Criminal process
*D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal* is associated with safeguards against custodial abuse. Where a question raises arrest, detention, access to counsel or self-incrimination, connect the factual issue to the specific constitutional and statutory protections rather than citing Article 21 alone.

## 8. Article 21A: right to education
Article 21A requires the State to provide free and compulsory education to children in the age group of six to fourteen years in the manner determined by law. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 gives statutory effect to this right. It is not accurate to state that Article 21A itself guarantees every form of education at every age without qualification.

## 9. Rights against exploitation: Articles 23–24

- **Article 23:** prohibits trafficking in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour, subject to the constitutional text. The prohibition can reach conduct by private persons as well as State actors. *People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India* is commonly studied in connection with forced labour and payment below minimum wages in the relevant context.
- **Article 24:** prohibits employment of children below fourteen years in factories, mines or other hazardous employment as stated by the Article. Other statutes may impose broader protections, so do not treat Article 24 as the entirety of child-protection law.

For a problem, identify the conduct, the vulnerable person, coercion/exploitation or prohibited work and the relevant legal source.

## 10. Freedom of religion: Articles 25–28
- **Article 25:** freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and the other constitutional provisions. The Article permits regulation in the stated contexts.
- **Article 26:** rights of religious denominations to manage religious affairs and institutions, subject to constitutional conditions.
- **Article 27:** limits compelling a person to pay taxes whose proceeds are specifically appropriated for promotion/maintenance of a particular religion or denomination, as the text provides.
- **Article 28:** regulates religious instruction/worship in educational institutions in the categories it describes.

Religious freedom is important but not absolute. Identify whether the question concerns individual conscience, religious practice, a denominational right, public funding/taxation or instruction in an institution. *Bijoe Emmanuel v State of Kerala* is a leading expression/faith case, and *Shirur Mutt* is significant for early Article 25–26 doctrine; check the precise facts and legal proposition before citing.

## 11. Cultural and educational rights: Articles 29–30
- **Article 29(1):** protects the right of a section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture to conserve it.
- **Article 29(2):** addresses denial of admission to State-maintained or State-aided educational institutions on the specified grounds.
- **Article 30:** protects religious and linguistic minorities' right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice, subject to constitutional doctrine and regulation that respects the right.

Do not treat Article 29 and Article 30 as identical or assume every educational regulation is either always valid or always invalid. *T.M.A. Pai Foundation* and *St. Xavier's College v State of Gujarat* are important references for minority educational institutions; explain the specific proposition from the judgment.

## 12. Article 32: constitutional remedies
Article 32 gives a right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Court may issue directions, orders or writs of the kinds identified in the Constitution.

| Writ | Basic purpose |
|---|---|
| Habeas corpus | Test the legality of detention and secure release where detention is unlawful |
| Mandamus | Require a public authority to perform a public/legal duty where the requirements are met |
| Prohibition | Prevent a lower court/tribunal from proceeding beyond jurisdiction in the applicable circumstances |
| Certiorari | Quash a decision/order for recognised jurisdictional or legal error in the applicable circumstances |
| Quo warranto | Test a person's legal authority to hold a public office |

Article 226 gives High Courts writ jurisdiction for Fundamental Rights and “for any other purpose” within its constitutional scope; it is broader in that respect than Article 32. Do not say High Court writs are automatically available whenever a person wants review; jurisdictional requirements and alternative-remedy doctrine can matter.

## 13. Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs)

DPSPs are in Part IV, Articles 36–51. Article 37 states that they are not enforceable by any court but are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country, and it is the State's duty to apply them in making laws.

They direct the State toward social and economic welfare, fair conditions, public health, education, environmental protection and other constitutional goals across the relevant Articles. The right exam point is that DPSPs guide legislation and interpretation but do not simply operate as the same kind of directly enforceable individual claim as a Fundamental Right.

### Fundamental Rights and DPSPs
In *State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), the Court gave effect to Fundamental Rights in conflict with the relevant directive, helping prompt constitutional amendments. Later doctrine seeks harmony between the two parts. *Minerva Mills v Union of India* (1980) emphasised constitutional balance and rejected an absolute primacy of one part that destroys the other.

**Good conclusion:** Fundamental Rights and DPSPs serve different constitutional roles; courts seek to interpret them harmoniously, subject to the text and basic-structure doctrine.

## 14. Fundamental Duties
Article 51A in Part IVA lists Fundamental Duties of citizens. They include respect for the Constitution and national symbols, cherishing the ideals of the freedom struggle, protecting sovereignty and unity, defending the country when called upon, promoting harmony and dignity of women, protecting cultural heritage and the environment, developing scientific temper, safeguarding public property and striving for excellence. The duty relating to educational opportunities for children was added by the 86th Amendment.

Fundamental Duties are not generally a standalone ground for punishment or a direct claim by a private person merely because another person failed to perform them. They can inform statutory interpretation, education and evaluation of public responsibilities. A specific statute may create an enforceable duty or offence, but identify that separate legal source.

## 15. High-value case map
- **Basic structure/Preamble:** *Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala* (1973).
- **Federalism and Article 356:** *S.R. Bommai v Union of India* (1994).
- **Article 14:** *E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu* (1974); *Maneka Gandhi* (1978).
- **Article 19(1)(a):** *Shreya Singhal v Union of India* (2015); *Romesh Thappar v State of Madras* (1950).
- **Article 20:** use cases matched to the exact clause; do not cite double-jeopardy cases for self-incrimination.
- **Article 21:** *Maneka Gandhi*; *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017); *D.K. Basu* (1997).
- **Articles 23–24:** *People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India* (1982).
- **DPSP balance:** *Champakam Dorairajan* (1951); *Minerva Mills* (1980).
- **Religion:** *Shirur Mutt* (1954); *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986).
- **Minority education:** *St. Xavier's College* (1974); *T.M.A. Pai Foundation* (2002).

## 16. How to write a strong constitutional answer
**Issue → Article text → doctrinal test → authority → fact-by-fact application → conclusion.**

For essay questions, add the constitutional context, evolution of doctrine, counterargument and limits. For problem questions, avoid repeating an entire case story: extract the ratio that controls the facts.
